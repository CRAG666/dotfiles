## Source C

### 10 Personal pronouns, names, titles

This chapter is also for native speakers of English.

This is an area of language usage that is complex and constantly evolving in line with societal changes. Several sections in this chapter should thus be seen as guidelines that may well become outdated. If you feel that I have misrepresented anything, then please contact me.

#### 10.1 *he/him/his, she/her/hers* vs *they*

The use of pronouns today in English is a sensitive topic. Below are some guidelines to help you avoid potentially offending someone.

Do not use *he/him/his* to refer to someone whose gender it is impossible for you to know and which in any case is not relevant. Instead use *they/them/their.*

- Someone rang the doorbell, but then **they /** ~~**he**~~ went away and I didn’t see who **they were /** ~~**he was**~~. … <small>We cannot use ‘he’ as we don’t know the gender of the person who rang the bell – we didn’t see the person.</small>
- When you arrive at the university we suggest that you register with a doctor. You will need to provide **them /** ~~**him**~~ with all your medical details. … <small>The doctor could be any gender.</small>
- A **doctor** plays a vital role in society, in fact **they / he** often … <small>The doctor could be any gender.</small>
- A **primary school teacher** can have a great influence on the future lives of **their** ~~/ **her**~~ pupils.
- <small>A teacher could be any gender.</small>

Traditionally, a singular subject (the *reader* in the example below) has always grammatically required a third person singular pronoun. This is no longer the case.

- Make sure **the user** can understand the instructions, because if **they /** ~~**he**~~ can’t **they /** ~~**he**~~ will not be able to use the machine.

An alternative solution to the example above, and which does follow traditional English grammar, is to use a plural form of the noun that the pronoun refers to.

- Make sure **users** can understand the instructions, because if **they** can’t **they** will not be able to use the machine.
- When **users** have connection problems, the system tries to reconnect **them** automatically.

#### 10.2 *he/she* vs *they* in academic texts

Only use *he* and *she* (and *his, him, her, hers*) when they are specifically used in relation to a male or female subject, respectively.

- Back in 2016, Barack Obama claimed in **his** speech that **he** … whereas in **her** speech Angela Merkel reaffirmed that **she** …
- The patient (**female**, 46 years old) had lost **her** arm in an accident.

Consider rephrasing sentences which contain *he/she* or *him/her*. Firstly, constantly writing *he/she* or *him/her* in a text can become quite heavy for the reader. Secondly, not everyone associates themself with one gender. In both cases, *they, them, their* are preferable.

- ~~When **a user** has connection problems, the system tries to reconnect **him/her** automatically. This means that **he/she** may have problems with **his/her** modem, and consequently **he/she** may need to … In such case **he/she** …~~
- When **a user has / users have** connection problems, the system tries to reconnect **them** automatically.
- When **users have** connection problems, the system tries to reconnect **them** automatically.

In some cases you may feel that using *they* rather than *he/she* sounds strange. In such situations, some authors prefer to use *he/she*. In the sentence below, *he* and *she* are not alternatives.

- When a child is born, **he/she** is often a great source of joy for the parents.

However, using *they* is now becoming more common.

- When a child is born, **they** are usually a great source of joy for the parents.

Be aware that machine translators and editing tools tend to be male-focused in terms of their translations with reference to certain jobs and professions, e.g. doctors will tend to be associated with the pronoun *he*, and nurses with *she*, professors with *he*, and teachers with *she*. See 11.9.

#### 10.3 Pronouns in emails, social media (e.g. LinkedIn), Zoom etc

A common practice today is to put your pronouns after your ‘signature’ in an email, or located under your name and photo on your LinkedIn profile, Zoom (and other conferencing apps) or other social media, or on your CV.

This is generally for one or more of reasons below:

1. you do not associate yourself with the binary genders (i.e. male, female) \*
2. you wish to show solidarity with LGTBTQ+ people, and create an inclusive space for normalizing these signatures to create equity among colleagues
3. because your name does not give a clear interpretation of whether you are male or female AND you wish to make this known in order for people to be able to address you correctly in your daily communication

Your choices are to put:

- *they / them* or *they / them* / their (for case 1 above)
- *she / her* or *she / her / hers* (for cases 2 and 3 if you wish to be identified as a woman)
- *he / him* or *he / him / his* (for cases 2 and 3 if you wish to be identified as a man)

Note: You are not obliged to follow this practice with reference to yourself. However, it is much more important when you are referring to others – see 10.6.

- <small>\* For a growing number of people it is very important that we move away from using male / female binaries and referencing these as the only two “genders.” It is thus considered that it is more inclusive to reference these genders as the “gender binaries” as this indicates that gender and sexuality are a spectrum of expression. In addition, some people feel that social norms have constrained these two “genders.”</small>

#### 10.4 CVs: names and preferred pronouns

Clearly, you cannot submit a CV without putting your name on it. Your name may or may not indicate your gender. In reality your gender should not be important in terms of your suitability for a job. In fact, in many countries by law you are not obliged to indicate your gender or reveal your marital status - either on your CV or in a job interview. Nor do you need to put a photograph of yourself.

However, you may in any case wish the professor, recruiter, human resources person, to know your gender. If your name does not clearly indicate your gender, or if it indicates a gender that you no longer associate with, then a simple solution is to put your pronouns after your name:

> Andrea Smith  
> (0044) 161 980 416 75  
> andrea.smith@uniman  
> Pronouns: *he/him/his*

An alternative is to use the cover letter to indicate your gender. In this case, you can put your pronouns under your signature.

> I look forward to hearing from you in the near future.  
> Best regards,  
> **Andrea Smith**  
> Pronouns: *he/him/his*

#### 10.5 Pronouns in the main text of a CV and in social media profiles

Many CVs / resumes today have an executive summary, where you summarize the main points of your career along with your knowledge areas. This summary is typically placed under your personal details in your CV, or located under your name in, for example, LinkedIn.

In the executive summary, and also in the other sections of your CV, the convention is not to use pronouns at all. This is not for political, social, or identity reasons, but simply so that you can be as concise as possible in order to make scanning and reading your CV as fast as possible for the reader.

- Expert knowledge in XYZ / ~~**She has**~~ **expert knowledge** …
- Attended XYZ conference in Vienna in … ~~**/ He attended**~~ …
- Three years’ experience in … / ~~**She has**~~ **three years’** …

On many profiles, you can also use the first person pronoun: *I*

- **I am** passionate about XYZ

For more details see Chapters 3 and 4 of *English for Academic CVs, Resumes, and Online Profiles*

#### 10.6 Non-binary pronouns

This section primarily refers to academic communication by anyone in contact with people from English-speaking countries such as the USA, Canada, Great Britain, Ireland, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand. The idea is that we should be aware that non-binary people may wished to be referred to with a particular pronoun.

To understand the meaning of ‘non-binary’, go to this Wikipedia page and from there choose the version in your language: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-binary_gender

##### in class

Not everyone feels that the gender that was assigned to them at birth corresponds to how they see their identity. On the basis of your perceptions of someone from the way they express gender, i.e. the way they look or the clothes they wear, you may instinctively want to use *he* or *she* when referring to them in the third person – for example when referring to a student in your class. However, this person may wish you to refer to them with a different pronoun. It is best to use “they” until you are given an indication of their pronoun preference.

When you introduce yourself, you can say:

- Hello. My name is Andrea and my pronouns are ‘he/his/him’.

The other person is then likely to respond in the same way.

You can also ask the class to use discussion posts / online class forums where everyone introduce themselves and gives their pronouns. This may make certain discussions easier. What is written in these introductions is helpful for both teachers and students to refer to in the future. Here, students can even write the phonetic spelling of their names- allowing for easier pronunciation/ proper pronoun usage in the classroom.

##### at conferences, visiting other universities or research centers

Today, when going to a conference or visiting a new university, you may need to pay attention when you talk about another person, e.g. a professor, a student, a researcher, to a third party A typical example is when you talk to one student about another student.

If you know in advance that you are going to meet a particular person or be talking about them, then you could

- look on their social or institutional profiles to see if they put their pronouns
- ask one of their colleagues in advance regarding which pronoun they use
- ask the person directly when you meet them

If none of the above are possible, then when you introduce yourself, you can say:

- Hello. My name is Andrea and my pronouns are ‘he/his/him’.

The other person is then likely to respond in the same way.

On other occasions, you may need to refer to someone who both you and your interlocutor know - as in the examples below.

- **Andrea** is a great student, **she** works very hard at **her** projects.
- I saw **Andrea** yesterday, **he** told me to send you **his** regards.
- Later this afternoon I’d like you meet **Andrea, they** have been working with me for the last two months.

All the above measures will help you navigate this very fluid area of modern life. They will enable you to use

- **he** for a man, or someone who identifies with being a male
- **she** for a woman, or someone who identifies with being a female
- **they** for a person who does not wish to be referred to as *he* or *she*, i.e. they do not identify with the gender binaries of *man* and *woman*

Important notes:

Some languages are not innately suited to dealing with these pronouns issue. However, when being addressed in their own language a person may be prepared to accept that they cannot be referred to with their preferred pronouns, but when being addressed in English they may expect that you adhere to ‘English rules’.

This usage of pronouns may seem very unfamiliar to you. But to others it is extremely important - it is their identity. You will generally be forgiven if you use the wrong pronoun when addressing a particular person. In fact, it is important to underline that if you wrongly assume someone else’s gender, but you correct yourself when it’s pointed out, then it should not be a problem at all. It’s just like mistaking a person’s name or its pronunciation when you don’t know them very well. In such cases, simply apologize. You can say:

- Thank you for telling me. I am sorry.

If you wish you could add a phrase such as:

- I am not a native speaker of English. / I was not aware of this usage. / In my language we do not have an equivalent, but of course I am willing to learn.

Unfortunately, the media have a tendency to only report cases, for example, where teachers, lecturers and professors have misused a student’s preferred pronoun and have then been severely reprimanded or even lost their job. Such cases are not the norm.

Nevertheless, it is important to make an effort to get it right, rather than deciding you can simply ignore the issue, or worse, make fun of it. If you continually use the wrong pronouns when you have been explicitly asked to use a different pronoun, then this could be seen as hostile and aggressive.

Using the correct pronoun goes some way to help foster inclusivity, equity, and a sense of belonging. It is also well known that research groups benefit from having diverse groups of people leading to more creativity, innovation, analysis and collaboration.

Initially, it may take you some time to get used to choosing the right pronoun, in future decades this is likely to become more and more automatic.

##### use of names

If you see someone’s first name printed on an official list, for example, a class register, then do not automatically assume that this how they wish to be called. They may no longer identify with the name they were given at birth. For instance, if someone was born with the name Lily (traditionally a female name), they may ask to be called “Eli” or some other non-binary and gender-neutral name. It is important respect their request.

#### 10.7 What to expect if your pronouns are *they / them / theirs*

Much of the academic world is not familiar with the use of *they / them / theirs* either as a neutral pronoun (*someone called but didn’t leave their name*) or as a choice not to conform to current usage by the LGBTQIA+ community and others.

Consequently it makes sense not to be offended if someone:

- addresses you by the wrong pronoun
- expresses ignorance, incredulity or bemusement when being informed about this particular use of these pronouns

For these people there are two issues. First, they may consider that such usage goes against the rules of grammar in their native language, so they are likely to struggle with what they perceive is a blatant misuse of grammar. Second, the sentiment behind the concept of non-binary may not fit well with their set of beliefs / cultural understandings.

#### 10.8 Using pronouns to reveal your gender when your gender may not be clear from your name

When you are living / studying outside your country of birth, people around you may not be able to understand your gender simply by looking at your name.

For a Westerner, *John Smith* is a traditional male name, but *Tao Pei Lin* would not be recognized instantly as a female name.

For several decades it has been common for students from China and nearby countries to adopt an English nickname in their correspondence in the academic world (and more recently on social media). For example, a female student that wishes to choose a nickname, will try to choose an English name which is non- ambiguously female, such as *Anna*, *Kathy*, etc.

This practice also follows what has been common in language classes (at least since the 1980s) where English is being taught as a second or foreign language. This enables the teacher to be able to communicate with the students more easily without risking to radically mispronounce their names.

Clearly not all Chinese people wish to adopt an English name, but may find themselves be addressed incorrectly as *Mr* or *Ms* in formal correspondence (note that in any case *Mr*, *Ms* and other titles are falling into disuse – see Sect 10.13).

To avoid this issue, some Chinese academics that have NOT adopted a nickname, when communicating with non-Chinese people, put their pronouns under their email signature and on sites such as Academia, ResearchGate and LinkedIn.

Such usage is thus simply based on convenience and likely has nothing to do with solidarity with non-binary people. At home in China, the same students do not put their pronouns, even though the Chinese language does have equivalents to the English pronouns. In cases where the gender is not known, the person will be referred to in Chinese as *that professor, that student* etc.

#### 10.9 Be careful when spelling other people’s names

Our names are very precious to us. Think of how you feel when someone misspells your name. You may probably feel some annoyance that someone has not taken the time to spell your name correctly.

This lack of attention could be interpreted both as a sign of disrespect and laziness. It could also reflect on your work: if you didn’t take the time to check the spelling of someone’s name, maybe you are the type of person who doesn’t check their results in an experiment, or who doesn’t repeat calculations to ensure they have the correct answer.

Many languages contain accents. These accents are also frequently found in the letters of people’s name. These accents are not an optional. To avoid making mistakes, cut and paste the person’s name into your document.

Accents and spelling conventions are also connected with people whose first language is English. For example, avoid the following mistakes.

Paul **McCartney** / ~~**Mccartney**~~ et al stated that all the world needs is love.

Sinead **O’Connor** / ~~**O’connor**~~ et al investigated the role of the Catholic faith in …

**Gráinne** / ~~**Grainne**~~ Ryan is the keynote speaker.

#### 10.10 Checking a person’s given name vs family name

When we see someone’s name written, we cannot always understand what gender they are. Also, we cannot understand which is their given name (first name) and which is their family name (surname, last name). For example, here are two names of Chinese people:

Tao Pei-Lin

Peng Lin

It might seem from the above that *Lin* could be a family name, and *Tao* and *Peng* are first names. In reality in the first case, *Pei-Lin* is the first name and *Tao* the family name. In the second case, *Peng* is the first name. What has happened is that the first case follows the usual Chinese order: family name + first name. In the second case, the student is studying in Europe and she has decided to use the Western formula: first name + family name. She could have also written *Lin, Peng*. In this case, the comma indicates that the word before the comma is her family name.

The solution is to copy the name as you find it on their official page of their university or on LinkedIn etc.

If you then wish to find out which is their first name, you can simply ask them. If you don’t ask them, then you may risk either being too formal or too informal when you refer to them or address them directly.

The moral of the story is that you cannot necessarily know what convention (in any language / culture) is being followed, so it is generally a good idea to ask, for example: *Sorry, what is your first name?*

#### 10.11 Anglo names

The convention in countries such as the USA, Canada, Ireland, the UK, Australia and New Zealand is to write your given name first and your family name second (this name is also known as your ‘last name’). Thus the author of this book is Adrian Smith, not Smith Adrian. Note also that some first names in English can be male or female. Adrian, for instance, is generally a male name, but can also be a female name.

Don’t automatically assume someone’s gender from their name. There are several gender neutral first names such as Brooklyn, Cameron, Charlie, Jamie, and Saxon. Gender neutral names are often popular chosen names, as they express a non-binary identification.

The family name may also be a common given name, e.g. *Henry James* (James is the family name not the first name).

However, because the convention in English is always: *given name + family name,* then generally you will not get confused between which is the family name and which is the given name.

#### 10.12 Showing respect by learning how to pronounce someone’s name

Make an effort to pronounce everyone’s name as best you can. By making an effort, you are also showing that person respect, particularly given the fact that they will have made an equal effort to learn the pronunciation of your name.

Your name may not be common in the country where you are currently living / studying. If you choose not to adopt an English nickname, then try and help your interlocutor to pronounce your name correctly. Think of common English words that rhyme with particular syllables in your name.

#### 10.13 Titles

There are certain titles that indicate someone’s gender and marital status and which were used when addressing people or addressing envelopes in the past, but are now rather antiquated.

Such titles are *Mr*, *Mrs, Miss* and *Ms*, which indicated at the time they were first used: *Mr* – a man (not specified whether married or not), *Mrs* – woman (married), *Miss* – woman (unmarried), *Ms* – woman (not specified whether married or not); *Ms* was originally designed to replace *Mrs* and *Ms*).

These titles fell into disuse decades ago, one reason being that it is certainly not necessary, for example, to know a woman’s marital status.

However, there are cases where some people in academia and industry, particularly in India, use the titles *Mr*, *Mrs* and *Miss/Ms* to indicate their gender in an email signature. For example:

- Ajay, N (Mr.)
- Kayalvizhi, S (Mrs.)

Here, Ajay and Kayalvizhi are the two people’s given names. The single capital letters (N and S) are the first letter of their family names. They have indicated their gender using *Mr* and *Mrs* - such usage is generally no longer the norm in other English speaking countries. What these examples highlight is that different cultures use different systems. If you are going to be communicating with such cultures it is worth investigating what norms they follow in order for you to be able to interpret what the convention is.

In any case, in academia *Mr*, *Mrs, Miss* and *Ms*, are not necessary. When addressing someone to their face you can show respect by using a title such as *Professor* or *Doctor*. *Doctor* is often used almost a generic formal form of address, even if the person does not actually have a PhD! When emailing them, again you can use *Professor* or *Doctor*.

Alternatively, if this is the first time you are writing to someone you have not previously met, you can use this formula *Dear first name + family name*. Here are some examples using my name (i.e. the author of this book that you are reading now).

- Dear Professor Smith
- Dear Dr Smith
- Dear Adrian Smith

If you do not know which is their first name and which is their family name, then simply use the order of names that you found on the web, or on their social media page.

- Dear Tao Pei Lin

Note that there are some titles that may exist in your own language that are not, however, used in English. For example we do not address someone who is a lawyer or engineer with a title indicating their profession.

- ~~Dear **Engineer / Lawyer** Smith~~
- Dear **Adrian / Dr / Doctor / Prof. / Professor** Smith.

#### 10.14 Addressing a group of people, addressing unknown people

You are likely to address a group of people or an unknown person on two occasions: at the beginning of a presentation and in an email.

Use gender-inclusive terms. The example in red excludes those who do not identify as either male or female. Non-binary people may feel marginalized by this wording, especially at a conference or in a work place.

- ~~Ladies and gentlemen, good morning. My name is … and …~~
- Good morning colleagues / everyone / attendees / all.

In an email, if you are writing to a group of people in, for example, your research team, you can write:

- Dear Colleagues
- Hi / Hello everyone

If you are writing to an individual whose name you don’t know or whose name is irrelevant (e.g. a helpdesk enquiry), then simply write *hello / hi,* or nothing at all.

- ~~Dear Sir or Madam~~
- Hi / Hello
- Dear Editor
