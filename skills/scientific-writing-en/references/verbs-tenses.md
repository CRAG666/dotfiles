### 5 Infinitive versus gerund (*-ing* form)

#### 5.1 Infinitive

Use the infinitive when you talk about the aim / purpose of an action, or how to carry something out. Do not precede the infinitive with *for.*

- ~~**To make / For to make / For making**~~ extra money, he designs and develops software.
- I need money **to buy** ~~**/ for buying**~~ a house

The negative infinitive is: *in order not to, so as not to.*

- **In order not to lose** ~~**/ For not losing / For don’t / To don’t lose**~~ data, make back-ups regularly.

*To* is not used after *make* (in active sentences) and *let.*

- The engine **makes** the wheels **go** ~~**/ to go**~~ round.
- Please **let** me **know** ~~**/ to know**~~ as soon as possible.

*To* is used after *make* in passive sentences.

- He was **made to write** ~~**/ made write**~~ the paper by his professor.

Most adjectives (including superlatives) are followed by the infinitive.

- It is **straightforward to verify** ~~**/ verifying**~~ that x = y.

When a quantifier (e.g. *enough, too much, too many, too little, too few*) is followed by a *noun + verb* construction, the verb is in the infinitive.

- It has been claimed that five users is **enough to catch** ~~**/ for catching**~~ 85% of the problems on the vast majority of websites.
- There are too few studies with too **few patients to determine** ~~**/ for determining**~~ which is the best drug.

The passive infinitive is formed by the verb *to be* + past participle.

- This enabled the ratio **to be calculated**.
- This enables us **to calculate** the ratio.
- The variants that influence these traits still need **to be identified**.
- We still need **to identify** the variants that influence these traits.

The perfect infinitive is formed by *to + have* + past participle. It is used when it is important to underline that something happened in the past, rather than being true on all occasions (normal infinitive).

- Around 10,000 people claim **to see** UFOs on a regular basis.
- Around 100,000 people claim **to have seen** a UFO last year.
- Malaria is estimated **to cause** almost one in five deaths in sub-Saharan Africa.
- This disease was estimated **to have caused** or contributed to death in 122 of the 5645 patients analyzed.

#### 5.2 *in order to*

Use *in order to* in formal situations.

- **In order to drive** a car, a license must be obtained.
- **To drive** a car you need a license.
- A dictionary was once vital **in order to learn** the difference between the various meanings of the same word, but not since the advent of ChatGPT.

Use *in order to* if one infinitive is immediately followed by another.

- If a scientist feels it necessary, therefore, **to publish** in English **in order to reach** a worldwide audience, does this mean that ...?

The use of *in order to* rather than simply *to* is often optional, and sometimes redundant.

- **\[In order\] to learn** English it helps to have a good teacher.
- **To learn** English it helps to have a good teacher.

*In order* is not necessary when the focus is on the activity rather than the purpose.

- **\[In order\] to teach** English, candidates are required to have a certificate.
- In the future, will there really be any need **to teach** English? AI will do everything.

#### 5.3 Gerund (*-ing* form): usage

The gerund is formed by adding *-ing* to the bare infinitive form (e.g. *study + ing = studying*). The negative is formed by putting *not* in front (e.g. *not studying, not working*).

Use the gerund when the verb is the subject of the sentence.

- **Developing** ~~**/ To develop**~~ software is their core business.

After a preposition, adverb or conjunction.

- Before **starting** ~~**/ to start**~~ up the PC, make sure it is plugged in.
- The contents may be displaced while **being** ~~**/ to be**~~ transferred.

Do not use the gerund when you are talking about an aim, objective or target. Instead, use the infinitive.

- Our aim is **to investigate** ~~**/ is investigating**~~ the use of X. <small>= **Investigating** the use of X is our aim.</small>
- The target **was to identify / was identifying** those elements that require X.<small>= **Identifying** those elements that require X was the target.</small>

#### 5.4 Ambiguity with the gerund

If you begin a sentence with the gerund, the reader may not be clear who or what this gerund refers to. Solution: rearrange the sentence using a *subject + verb* construction.

- ~~**Being equal** for all the transients **the frequency spectrum**, the curves have the same shape and differ only in the amplitude.~~
- Since **the frequency spectrum is equa**l for all the transients, the curves have the same shape and differ only in amplitude.
- ~~**Setting the status** to OFF, users will not be able to operate the machine.~~
- If **the status is set** to OFF, users will not be able to operate the machine.
- ~~After **washing** to remove impurities, **the gels** were incubated for 90 min.~~
- After **the gels had been washed** to remove impurities, they were incubated for 90 min.

When the gerund appears in the second part of a sentence it may not be clear if it refers to the subject or the object of the verb in the first part. Solution: Use *that* or *because* (*since, as* etc.) to clarify.

- ~~Professor Yang only teaches students **having** a good level of English.~~ <small>Who has good English – the students or Yang?</small>
- Professor Yang only teaches students **that have** a good level of English. <small>It is clear that it is the students who have the good level of English.</small>
- ~~Suzi teaches students **having** a passion for teaching.~~ <small>Who has passion – the students or Suzi?</small>
- Suzi teaches students **since / because** she has a passion for teaching.

If you are simply giving additional information, use *and* or begin a new sentence.

- ~~This document gives an overview of X, **throwing** light on aspects of ...~~
- This document gives an overview of X **and throws** light on aspects ...
- This document gives an overview of X. **It thus throws** light on aspects ...

The gerund can be ambiguous when the reader is not sure whether you are using it in a restrictive (i.e. only a particular subset of cases) or non-restrictive sense (i.e. all cases). Instead, use *that* or *which* to clarify.

- ~~Phenolic resin components (PRCs) **occurring** on the surfaces of plant organs have been frequently used.~~ <small>Does this mean all or just some PRCs? It is no clear.</small>
- Phenolic resin components (PRCs) **that occur** on the surfaces of plant organs have been frequently used. <small>Not all PRCs occur on plant organs.</small>
- Phenolic resin components (PRCs)**, which occur** on the surfaces of plant organs, have been frequently used. <small>All PRCs occur on plant organs, this is just additional information. Note the comma before *which*.</small>

In some cases the best solution is to rearrange the sentence.

- ~~A horizontal force is applied to one cylinder at a constant rate **measuring** the corresponding displacement.~~
- A horizontal force is applied to one cylinder at a constant rate. **This rate measures** the corresponding displacement.
- A horizontal force is applied to one cylinder at a constant rate. **This force** is then used to measure the corresponding displacement.

#### 5.5 *by* vs *thus* + gerund to avoid ambiguity

Use the gerund at the beginning of the sentence only when it is the subject of the main verb. Otherwise use *by + ing*. In the first example below *learning* is the subject, in the second *you* is the subject.

- **Learning** English will help **you** to pass the exam.
- **By learning** ~~**/ Learning**~~ English **you** will pass the exam. <small>= If you learn English you will pass the exam.</small>
- **By clicking** ~~**/ Clicking**~~ on the mouse you can open the window. <small>= If you click ...</small>

Use *thus* plus the gerund to indicate the consequence of doing something.

- We learn English **thus enabling** ~~**/** English **enabling**~~ us to communicate with our international colleagues. <small>= We learn English and thus we can communicate ...</small>
- The introduction of the euro led to a rise in prices **thus causing** ~~/ prices **causing**~~ inflation.
- <small>= The introduction of the euro led to a rise in prices *and this caused inflation.*</small>

Note the difference in meaning created by using *by* rather than *thus*, and vice versa.

- This improves performance **by keeping** customers satisfied.<small>= Performance improves when customers are satisfied.</small>
- This improves performance **thus keeping** customers satisfied. <small>= If performance improves then customers will be satisfied.</small>

#### 5.6 Prefer *which* or *that* to the -*ing* form

Be careful of using the *-ing* form when it is not 100% clear whether the *-ing* form is being used in a restrictive or non-restrictive sense.

- ~~Edible **jellyfish belonging** to the order *Rhizostomeae* are a popular seafood in Asia.~~ <small>Not clear if the author is referring to all jellyfish or just a subset.</small>
- Edible **jellyfish, which** belong to the order *Rhizostomeae*, are a popular seafood in Asia. <small>All jellyfish are *Rhizostomeae.*</small>
- **The** / **Those edible jellyfish that** belong to the order *Rhizostomeae* are a popular seafood in Asia. <small>Only some jellyfish are *Rhizostomeae.*</small>
- ~~Many authors have performed studies **comparing** X and Y.~~ <small>Not clear if the subject of the studies is a comparison of x and y, or if the authors conducted the studies by comparing x and y.</small>
- Many authors have performed studies **by comparing** X and Y. <small>The authors compared X and Y in order to make their study.</small>
- Many authors have performed studies **that compare** X and Y. <small>The authors conducted a comparison of x and y in their study.</small>

Even where there is no ambiguity, the *-ing* form can be replaced with *that.* This is the case when the *-ing* form has been used to define the previous noun.

- These are complexes formed by simple ligands **containing / that contain** a maximum of five coordinating centers.

The use of *having* tends to be confined to mathematics, physics and computer science. Essentially, you can always use *that has* or *that have.*

- Markov processes **having / that have** a countable state space are known as ...
- The null set is the set **having / that has** no elements.
- A person **that has** ~~**/ having**~~ no job is called ‘unemployed’.

#### 5.7 Verbs: infinitive vs gerund

Verbs that express purpose / objective + infinitive : *afford*, *attempt*, *choose*, *compel*, *convince, decide, encourage, force, hope, intend, invite, learn, manage, neglect, oblige, offer, order, plan, persuade, prefer, promise, propose, refuse, remember, study, teach, try, want, warn, wish, would like.*

Verbs that express appearance + infinitive*: appear, seem.*

- We are **planning to have** ~~**/ having**~~ a meeting next week.
- I **write to inform** ~~**/ informing**~~ you that your invoice has now been processed.

Verbs that are followed by some kind of activity or course of action tend + gerund, e.g. *avoid, carry on, consider, contemplate, delay, entail, finish, imagine, imply, mean, miss, postpone, recommend, risk*, *suggest.*

- The survey also showed that 88% of these graduates were satisfied with their programs of study and would **recommend studying** ~~**/ to study**~~ in Scotland.
- Tagawaki et al. have **suggested doing** ~~**/ to do**~~ this in reverse order.
- This **entails carrying** ~~**/ to carry**~~ out further tests.
- We have **finished writing** ~~**/ to write**~~ the first draft.

*Prevent* and *stop* are followed by an *object + gerund* construction.

- Does parental disapproval **prevent teenagers from drinking** ~~**/ to drink**~~ alcohol?
- ~~How do we **stop doctors to overprescribe** antibiotics?~~
- How do we **stop doctors \[from\] overprescribing** antibiotics?

Use the gerund after *to* in these verbs: *be dedicated to, be devoted to, be an aid to, look forward to, contribute to, object to.*

- Most of this section is **devoted to reviewing** ~~**/ devoted to review**~~ the literature.
- I look **forward to hearing** ~~**/ forward to hear**~~ from you.

When a recommendation or suggestion is made to a third party, then use the following construction: *recommend* / *suggest* that someone \[*should*\] do (infinitive form) something.

- The referees **recommend / suggest that you / she \[should\] reorganize** the structure of your / her paper.
- We **recommend / suggest that** policy changes in this direction **\[should\] be made.**

*start* and *begin* can be followed by either the infinitive or gerund with no apparent change in meaning. However if *start* and *begin* are in a continuous form (e.g. *is starting, was beginning*), then they are followed by the infinitive.

- I have **started to learn / learning** Spanish.
- I am **starting to learn** ~~**/ learning**~~ Spanish.

#### 5.8 Verbs that take both infinitive and gerund, but with a difference in meaning

Sometimes a verb takes the infinitive when the focus is on the purpose or objective, but when the focus is on the activity the same verb takes the gerund.

- The experiments on the animals **were stopped in order to avoid** any further protests by activists.
- We **stopped doing** the experiments to avoid protests by animal activists.
- Please **remember to include** your biography with your manuscript.
- The patient **remembered dreaming** about his mother the night before.
- We **regret to inform** you that we cannot accept your proposal.
- I **regretted not accepting** the job proposal.
- We would **like to emphasize** that ...
- I **like playing** all kinds of sports.

#### 5.9 Verbs that require person / thing + infinitive

Some verbs when used in the active require a direct object before the infinitive. There are three typical constructions, all with the same meaning.

Some common verbs that follow all three rules (\*means they only follow Rules 1 and 2): *advise, ask, encourage, force, oblige, offer\*, promise\*, prefer\*, request, want\*, wish\*, would like\*; allow, enable, permit, predict, expect, forecast*

In the three rules below, *allow* is just an example verb.

1) X allows Y to do Z.

- The build-up of large water masses against the shore **forces** the water **to move** ~~**/ forces the water moving**~~ seaward as an undertow.
- ~~A passport **permits traveling / to travel** across national borders.~~
- A passport **permits** the holder **to travel** across national borders.
- ~~The referees **want / have asked / have requested we make** various changes.~~
- The referees **want / have asked / have requested us to make** various changes.

2) X allows Y to be done.

- This software **allows tasks to be carried out** ~~**/ allows to carry out tasks**~~ more quickly.
- The editors **expect the changes to be made** ~~**/ expect that the changes are made**~~ before the end of the month.
- I **would like** you **to make** ~~**/ that you make**~~ the following changes:

3) X is allowed to do Y.

- Ph.D. students **are encouraged to present** posters at the conference.
- With this password **users enable to use** the system.

#### 5.10 Active and passive form: with and without infinitive

The infinitive is used after the passive form (but not after the active) with the following verbs: *assume, believe, hypothesize, imagine, suppose, think*. These verbs all express some kind of opinion or reasoning.

When the verbs listed above are used in the active, a different construction is required (*that* + noun + verb in active form).

- The value of x **is assumed to be** equal to 1.
- We **assume** that the value of x **is** ~~**/ to be**~~ equal to 1.
- They believe **they have found** ~~**/ to have found**~~ the answer.
- It **was assumed** that the problem had been resolved.
- We **assumed that we had** ~~**/ assumed to have**~~ already resolved this problem.

The following verbs are NOT followed by the infinitive in the active form: *believe, realize, think*. Instead use this formula: *verb + (that) + pronoun + suitable tense*

- We **believe** (**that) we are** ~~**/ believe to be**~~ the first to have revealed this discrepancy.
- We **realized** (**that) we had** ~~**/ realized to have**~~ this problem only a month ago.
- She thought (**that) she was** ~~**/ to be**~~ right.

#### 5.11 Imperative form

The imperative is formed with the infinitive without *to*. It is used in manuscripts in order to do the following.

Remind the reader of certain information, or bring attention to certain facts.

- **Recall** that x=1.
- **Note** that the values of x may vary.

Give hypotheses. Note us *let us* and not *let’s* (too informal).

- \[Let us\] **Suppose** that x=1.
- **Let** x be equal to 1.

Refer the reader to other sections in the paper or external documents.

- This is of great importance (**see** below).
- **See** Smith’s paper \[23\] for details.

### 8 Modal verbs: *can, may, could, should,* *must* etc.

#### 8.1 Present and future ability and possibility: *can* versus *may*

*Can* indicates a characteristic behavior. When certain conditions are met or desired, *can* indicates that things are possible but do not necessarily happen.

*May* indicates only the potential for something to happen. It indicates uncertainty and is thus used to make hypotheses, to speculate about the future, or to talk about probability.

- I **can** see <small>\[= I am free\]</small> you tomorrow – what time shall we meet?
- I **may** <small>\[= perhaps\]</small> be here tomorrow, but I am not 100% sure.
- Bilinguals are people that **can** <small>\[= are able\]</small> speak two languages.
- Bilinguals **may** <small>**\[=** sometimes, but not always\]</small> have learning difficulties when very young.
- Government cuts in education funding **can** have <small>\[= usually have\]</small> devastating effects on research.
- In the next decade such government cuts **may** <small>\[= possibly\]</small> lead to the closure of several universities.
- This situation **can** be <small>\[= This situation is\]</small> quite dangerous when hydrogen is present in the chamber. Such dangers **can** be mitigated by properly designing the compartments.
- It **may** <small>\[= it is possible that is\]</small> be dangerous to speculate about the possibilities of this actually happening as so many factors are involved.
- It **can** rain <small>\[= It rains\]</small> a lot during a monsoon, up to 20 cm of rain at one time.
- It usually rains a lot during a monsoon, but this year it **may** <small>\[= possibly, but not necessarily\]</small> rain less as a result of global warming.

*May have + past participle* is used to indicate a deduction made about a past event. Note: the form ~~*can have + past participle*~~ does not exist in the affirmative.

- Our sample was only small. Clearly, this **may** ~~**/ can**~~ **have affected** the results.

When *can* and *may* are used in the affirmative form there is often little or no difference in meaning. However, *can* indicates greater certainty and is therefore preferred in definitions (last blue example below).

- In our view, having two systems **can** ***/*** **may** be a more reliable way for dealing with this problem.
- In our view, having two systems **can** ***/*** **may** be a more reliable way for dealing with this problem.
- Dogs **can** ***/*** **may** eat up to 5 kg of food per day, as can be seen in Table 4.
- Dogs **can** ***/*** **may** eat up to 5 kg of food per day, as can be seen in Table 4.
- A university **can** be defined <small>\[= is defined, has been defined\]</small> as a place of advanced learning.
- A university **may** be defined as ‘a place of advanced learning’, but in reality not much learning takes place at my university.

#### 8.2 Impossibility and possibility: *cannot* versus *may not*

*Cannot* indicates impossibility (i.e. a certain event or scenario is not possible).

*May not* indicates there is a possibility that something will not happen (i.e. a certain event or scenario is not likely).

- I apologize, but I **cannot** come to the meeting as I will be in Hong Kong.
- I **may not** be able to come to the meeting tomorrow – is it alright if I let you know later today?
- It is well known that most North Americans and Britons **cannot** speak <small>\[= do not speak\]</small> any foreign languages.
- Professor Smith is English, so he **may not** ~~**/ cannot**~~ speak any foreign languages.

*Cannot have* + past participle indicates a deduction regarding the impossibility of a past event.

*May (not) have* + past participle is used to speculate about the past, particularly in the Discussion; *might have* and *could have* can also be used in the same way.

- Shakespeare was not born until 1564 so this work (dated 1560) **cannot** ~~**/ may not**~~ have been written by him.
- Although our sample was only small, this **may not** ~~**/ cannot**~~ have affected the results because the sample was, in any case, very representative.

#### 8.3 Ability: *can, could* versus *be able to, manage, succeed*

*Can* is used to talk about a future ability provided that the decision is being made now. Note: ~~*will can*~~ is not correct.

- I **can** ~~**/ will can**~~ finish the paper by tomorrow.

In cases where *can* is not possible to talk about a future event, a form of *be able to* is generally used.

- I **will be able to** ~~**/ can**~~ speak better English when I have finished this course.

*Could* in the affirmative indicates a habitual past ability, i.e. something that someone or something was able to do regularly; *was able to* can also be used in this context.

- The patient **could** ***/*** **was able to** walk at the age of six months.

When describing an ability to do something on one particular past occasion, *could* is never used in the affirmative and interrogative forms. In both these cases, use a form of *to be able to, to succeed in* or *to manage.*

- I **managed** ***/*** **was able to** ~~**/ could**~~ finish the manuscript on time. = I **succeeded** in finishing the …

In order to avoid confusion with the conditional, to talk about past inabilities it is better to replace *could not* with *did not manage to, did not succeed in,* or *was / were not able to.*

- They **couldn’t** do it. potentially ambiguous (now? or on an occasion in the past?)
- They **didn’t manage** ***/*** **were unable to** do it.
- They **didn’t succeed** in doing it.

#### 8.4 Deductions and speculations about the present: *must,* *cannot, should*

*Must* is used for a drawing logical conclusions that are affirmative; *have to* is not generally used in such contexts (too informal).

- If X = 1 and Y = then X + Y **must** ~~**/ has to**~~ equal three.

*Cannot* is used for drawing logical conclusions that are negative.

- If X = 1 and Y = then X + Y **cannot** ~~**/ must not**~~ equal five.

*Should* indicates what is likely (but not certain) to happen.

- If the two substances are mixed together, they **should** ~~**/ must**~~ go red. However, occasionally the mixture is brown.

#### 8.5 Deductions and speculations: *could, might (not)*

*Could* is often used to suggest a possible course of action.

*Might* indicates a possible / uncertain reaction to or consequence of a course of action.

- One solution **could** be to get parents and children to swap roles for a day.
- What if parents and children swapped roles for a day? How **might** they behave differently?
- Future research **could** be directed towards elucidating this pathology.
- Such research **might** then reveal the true causes of this pathology.

Sometimes *could* and *might* can be used interchangeably.

- The temperature then rises dramatically. This effect **could** ***/*** **might** be due to … and this **could** ***/*** **might** explain why …

*Could not* is NOT used to make speculations. Instead use *cannot* to indicates impossibility.

- This **cannot** ~~**/ could not**~~ be the reason why the first two experiments gave very different results. There must be another reason …

*Might not* means that there is a possibility that something is not true.

- This **might (may) not** be the reason why the first two experiments gave very different results. There is a possibility that there are other explanations …

#### 8.6 Present obligations: *must, must not, have to, need*

*Must* and *must not* are not frequently found in papers, but are often found in specifications or instruction manuals. Forms of *to have to* are rare in papers.

*Must* means that something is an absolute requirement given by a specific authority. Note: *to have to* is not generally used in such circumstances.

- Helmets **must** be worn on the building site at all times.

*To have to* is used to report what an external authority has decided.

- You **have to** wear a helmet on the building site at all times.
- I think we **have to** fill out the form and then sign it.
- The form **must** be filled out and signed by the applicant. <small>= Please ensure that the form is filled out by the applicant.</small>

*Must not* means that something has been prohibited by an authority.

*Do / does not have to* means that something is not mandatory. The forms ~~*hasn’t to*~~, ~~*haven’t to*~~ and ~~*hadn’t to*~~ are incorrect.

- Authors **must not** copy the text of other authors.
- As a Ph.D. student, I have to write a dissertation in my third year. However, I **don’t have to** write it in English – I also have the option of writing it in my own language.

*Need* indicates necessity and may be used to make a recommendation.

- This area **needs** further investigation.

In general English*, do / does not need* mean approximately the same as *do / does not have to*.

- We **don’t need to** do it tomorrow; we can do it next week if you want.
- <small>= We **don’t have to** do it tomorrow; we can do it next week if you want.</small>

#### 8.7 Past obligation: *should have* + past participle, *had to, was supposed to*

*Must* has no past form. When you refer to a past obligation that was fulfilled (i.e. you did what you were obliged to do), use *had to.* Use *didn’t have to* when there was no obligation to do something.

- We **had to** ~~**/ musted**~~ perform six experiments to ensure repeatability.
- We **didn’t have to** ~~**/ hadn’t to**~~ do an oral exam, just a written one.

*Was / were supposed to* is used to refer to something that you were obliged to do in order to comply with some authority, but in reality did not do.

- The manuscript **was supposed to have been completed /** ~~**had to be completed**~~ last week, but unfortunately they are still working on it.

*Should have* + past participle is used to refer to something that you did not do, but it would have been better if you had done it.

- We **should have sent** ~~**/ had to send**~~ the Abstract to the conference, then we could have presented our research. Now we can only go and watch. <small>= We did not send an abstract.</small>

*Was going to* is used to refer to what you were planning to do, but did not do.

- I **was going to send** ~~**/ had to send**~~ my Abstract to the conference organizers, but I forgot.
- <small>= We did not send an abstract.</small>
- We **had to send** an Abstract to the conference organizers, let’s hope it is accepted. <small>= We sent an abstract.</small>

#### 8.8 Obligation and recommendation: *should*

In non-technical English, *should* is used to give friendly recommendations and to express opinions.

- You **should** ~~**/ must**~~ use ChatGPT – it is very efficient!
- I think the third world debt **should** ~~**/ must**~~ be cancelled.

*Should (not)* is used to make strong recommendations (rather than giving direct orders).

- Special glasses **should** be worn in the lab. Computers **should not** be turned off without first being prepared for shutdown.

*Should* is often found in the Conclusions section, when the authors give their recommendations to other authors regarding possible directions for future work.

- We suggest that future work **should** ~~**/ must**~~ address the need to …

To avoid sounding arrogant, be careful how you use *should* when saying how your findings, applications, methodologies might be useful for other people – prefer *may.* Alternatively, precede your affirmation with *we believe that.*

- ~~Those working in the field of medicine **should** also use our approach.~~
- Our approach **may / might** also be useful for those working in the field of medicine.

### 14 Tenses: present and past

#### 14.1 Present simple vs present continuous

present simple - actions or situations that happen repeatedly, all the time, or at any time, including established scientific facts and findings, theorems, definitions, lemmas, proofs etc.

present continuous - trends and situations that are evolving now (trends), or programmed future events.

- It is well known that in many universities how much you **write** (i.e. the quantity) is often considered to be more important than how well you write (i.e. the quality).
- At the moment **we are writing** a paper on …
- Some maps of the world’s oceans **show** the widths of the continental shelves.
- The patients **are now showing** signs of fatigue, whereas before they were full of energy.
- The container **holds** the samples.
- The conference **is being held** in July.

In research papers, the present continuous tends to be only used to describe a current trend. In the red examples below, the meaning is not something that is happening now but something that is generally true or that can be deduced from a diagram or table.

- The molecule, whose concentration **changes** ~~**/ is changing**~~ depending on a reduction in oxygen availability, is …
- However, the transcriptome changes **only partially overlap** ~~**/ are only partially overlapping**~~ with the anaerobic ones.
- The G box is usually associated with light-regulated genes and **does not influence** ~~**/ is not influencing**~~ gene expression under hypoxia.

#### 14.2 Past simple vs present perfect

Use the past simple when the action is clearly past.

- This building technique **was exploited** ~~**/ has been exploited**~~ by the ancient Egyptians for the pyramids.

Use the past simple when there a clear time reference (e.g. *in 2023*, *last month, three years ago*).

- In 2024 Carter **suggested** ~~**/ has suggested**~~ that complex sentences could also lead to high levels of stress for the reader \[Carter, 2024\].
- Smith ~~**first used / has first used**~~ this procedure more than a decade ago.

#### 14.3 Present perfect: Abstracts

present perfect – to state the background situation as a basis for current research.

- The sea level **has changed** throughout the Earth’s history and will continue to do so. The sea level **changes** every year.
- Over the last 60 years English **has transformed** itself from a predominantly writer-oriented language to a reader-oriented language.

The active form of the present perfect is often used in an Abstract to announce a new finding or some new advance in a particular discipline. This usage tends to be confined to the first line of the Abstract, or immediately after the background information has been given (first pair of examples below).

- We **have developed** a new system for converting wind into energy.
- Dementia **is** an increasingly common problem in advanced societies and **is** known to cause … We **have discovered** a treatment for dementia.

To add further details about this innovation/news, the present simple or past simple are used.

- Our system works by harvesting wind from … We **implemented** the system in a wind farm in …
- This treatment **consists** of … It **was tested** on a sample of 543 patients aged over 80.

Do not use the passive form of the present perfect in an Abstract to describe what you have done in your research. This is because the passive form does not tell the reader who carried out the action, so it is ambiguous. Thus the reader could interpret the action as having been done by someone else at some other time.

- ~~A new approach to solving x, y and z **has been discovered**.~~ <small>first sentence in an abstract. Reader does not know who made the discovery – the authors of this paper or someone else previously.</small>
- We **have discovered** a new approach …
- This paper **presents** a new approach …

#### 14.4 Present perfect: Introduction

The Introduction of a paper presents the state of the art in your field by looking back into the past to previous papers on the same subject as your paper. The present perfect thus often features in the Introduction because this tense links the past with the present.

- Since 2023 there **have been** many other attempts to establish an international readability index \[Smith et al, 2023; Smithson 2022\], but until now no one **has managed** to solve the issue of …
- Establishing an international readability index **represents** a frequent topic in the literature \[Kim et al, 2029; Li 2022\]. The essential problem **is** how to solve the issue of …

If there is no past-present reference, then use the present simple to indicate a habitual situation.

- If language **transforms** our thinking, **do specific languages transform** it in different ways? Since the 2010s / For the last few decades, English writers **have published** several thousand books.

Use the present continuous for actions taking place now or in the current period of time.

- English writers typically **publish** their work in English, but more and more **are now publishing** in other languages too.

#### 14.5 Present perfect: refering to the literature

present perfect to give past-to-present background information. The present simple is possible here, but is much less commonly used.

- Many authors \[6, 8, 12\] **have claimed** / **claim** that there is life on Mars.

present perfect if the method, technique, procedure etc is the subject of the verb and there is no time reference

past simple if the author is the subject of the verb. In such cases there may or may not be a time indication.

- This method **has been used** to investigate both problems \[24\].
- Smith **used** this method to investigate both problems \[24\].
- This procedure **has been exploited** by many authors in order to conduct very diverse investigations.
- Smith first **used** this procedure in 2023 \[24\].
- In support of such treatment, Griggs **has made** the surprising discovery that …
- Recently, Griggs **made** the surprising discovery that …

past simple to refer to the literature when you give the date within the main sentence (i.e. not just in parentheses). However, if you then mention a series of papers that have been published up to the present day (and possibly in the future), then use the present perfect.

- In 2023, Don Na et al \[25\] **suggested** that women are superior to men. This **has subsequently proved** many other authors (Femme et al, 2026; Mujer, 2027; Kobieta, 2028; and Jo Sei et al, 2029\].

past simple (or less commonly present simple) to report what other authors have *suggested, proposed, claimed, implied, hypothesized, put forward* etc, There cannot be a specific time reference if you choose to use the present simple in such cases.

- For instance in \[5\], the authors **suggested / suggest** that a new strategy could be introduced to …

#### 14.6 Present perfect: Conclusions

present perfect (not past simple) in the Conclusions to summarize what you have done in the paper - the focus here is on the writing and construction of the paper (typical verbs: *present, show, describe, explain, outline*).

- Conclusions: We **have presented** a new methodology for teaching English. We **have shown** that … We **have described** three cases where …
- ~~Conclusions: We **presented** a new methodology for teaching English. We **showed** that … We **described** three cases where …~~ <small>It seems like you are talking about one of your previous papers.</small>

#### 14.7 Present perfect: rebuttal letter

When writing a response to the referees regarding your manuscript, use the present perfect to announce what additions and changes you have made. However, when you give more details of these changes and why you made them, use the past simple.

- We **have added** a new figure … Table 3 **has been deleted**.
- The Conclusions **have been completely rewritten**.
- The figure **was added** because … Table 3 **was** in fact unnecessary. We **decided** to rewrite the conclusions on the basis of Ref 3’s comments.
- We **opted** to keep Table 3 because …

#### 14.8 Present perfect: problem areas

present perfect (not present simple) when, with reference to a present situation, you state how long (in days, years, months etc) this situation has been operative.

- I **have been** here for a week. <small>I arrived one week ago and I am still here.</small>
- I **am** here for a week. <small>I arrived today (or recently) and I will stay seven days.</small>
- ~~I **am** here since / from a week.~~

present perfect (not present simple) to state that this is the first (second, third, etc) time that something has been done.

- We **have used** / ~~We **use**~~ this system for many years, since 2021 in fact.
- ~~They are many years that we **use** this system.~~
- ~~We **use** this system since many years.~~
- We **have not used** this equipment for several months.
- ~~We **do not use** this equipment from several months.~~
- We **have been /** ~~**are**~~ here since last Monday.
- It is the first time that we **have used** ~~**/ we use**~~ this system.
- This is only the second time that such a result **has been published** ~~**/ result is published**~~ in the literature.

present perfect when *once* and *as soon as* mean ‘after something has been done’.

- Once/As soon as the initial tests **have been made**, the research effort will focus on …

present simple when you are talking about situations that are generally true.

- Generally speaking, once you **start** reading the book, you soon become addicted.

If a situation has existed for a considerable number of years then the present perfect is preferred to present perfect continuous,

If the situation is more recent then both forms can be used with the continuous form indicating that the situation may be temporary.

- For thousands of years humans **have tried** to give a meaning to life.
- For several years researchers in this field **have been trying** to understand the …

Do not use the present perfect continuous for completed actions, or for when you talk about the number of occasions that something has happened, or when you specify a quantity \[exception: *years, days, hours, minutes* etc\].

- We **have already written** three papers on this topic.
- We **have been investigating** this problem for three years.

#### 14.9 Past simple vs present simple: aims and procedures

To outline main topics of the research and aims of a project: use the present simple in the first sentence to describe the whole paper, use the past simple to outline what you did.

- This paper **outlines** a methodology for establishing the amount of verbosity in a nation’s language.
- To establish our verbosity index, we **analyzed** five languages. We **classified** these languages in terms of x, y, z. On the basis of these results, we then **calculated** the number of …

When describing the aim of the project, use the present simple if the project is still ongoing, use the past simple if your paper describes a finished project.

- The aim of this research **is** to …
- The aim of the project **was** to …

present simple to describe a procedure (method etc) established by other authors, i.e. to state general principles relevant to the procedure.

- A cloze procedure **is** a technique in which words **are deleted** from a text according to a wordcount formula. The passage **is presented** to students, who then …
- In our procedure the students **are first split** into groups by age and level. This grouping **enables** the teacher to …

present simple to refer to your methodology, process or procedure if you are just explaining in general how it works, rather than what you did on one specific occasion.

- Our methodology **consists** of the following steps: First, we **gather** the data. Second, we **sort** the data by …

past simple to report what the objectives of your experiments were, what equipment was used, how other methods were adapted, what steps were followed etc.

- The aim of our procedure **was** to find a way for teachers to place students into groups. We **used** GroupSoft (GS Inc, USA) which automatically *places* students into groups. We **adapted** the software by adding an additional step in which students *are* preliminary grouped by age.
- In the second experiment we **proceeded** as follows: First, we **gathered** the data. Second, we **sorted** the data by …

#### 14.10 Present simple vs past simple: Results and Discussion

When you use the simple present to report your findings it MUST be 100% clear to readers that you are talking about YOUR findings and NOT what has been reported in the literature. If it is not clear, your sentence will be ambiguous.

- ~~We found that green and red **produces** white. This only **seems** to happen when the ratio of green to red **is** 6:1. But when the ratio **is** 4: this **produces** yellow.~~ <small>The reader may think that ‘seems to happen’ and ‘produces’ refer to what other people have found.</small>
- We found that green and red **produced** white. This only **seemed** to happen when the ratio of green to red **was** 6:1. But when the ratio **was** 4: yellow **was** produced. <small>The reader is clear that you are only talking about what you found.</small>

present simple to state what a figure or table shows, highlights, describes, reports etc.

- The results are given in Table 2 which **shows** that … In addition, Figure 1 **highlights** that X equals Y.

present simple to discuss your data and results, and to state the implications of your findings. Typically after *show, explain, highlight, believe, mean, indicate, reveal*.

- We believe that this means that our method **outperforms** all previous methods.

Introductory verbs such as *show, highlight, reveal* can be either in the present simple or past simple, but to talk about what you *found, discovered, noticed* etc only use the past simple.

- These results **highlight / highlighted** the importance of carrying out tests in triplicate.
- We found that best results **were achieved** by carrying out tests in triplicate.

#### 14.11 Past continuous and past perfect vs simple past

past continuous to indicate a long action that was interrupted by a short action. Use the past simple for the short action.

- We **encountered** a problem while we **were loading** the software.

past continuous to indicate two long actions that took place at the same time.

past simple for a series of non-simultaneous actions.

- While I **was studying** I **was also working** full time.
- We **downloaded** the software and **installed** it.
- I **studied** at Ulaanbaatar State University. I then **worked** for Mongolian Railways.

past perfect to highlight when one action took place earlier than a later action.

- Two dogs that **had died** for reasons unrelated to this study were used to characterize the approach.
- Unfortunately, one of the cats **died** during the experiments.

Consider rewriting sentences where the past perfect is embedded in a relative clause (i.e. a clause that begins with *which*).

- ~~The ethyl acetate phase, *which* ***had been dried*** *under a gentle stream of nitrogen and which had been subjected to a series of dilutions (in the form of x, y and z),* was re-dissolved with 50 mL of the eluent B.~~
- The ethyl acetate phase was first subjected to a series of dilutions in the form of x, y and z. It **was then dried** under a gentle stream of nitrogen. Finally, it was then re-dissolved with 50 mL of eluent.

### 15 Tenses: future, conditional, passive

English has many ways to express the future. However, only *will* and the present continuous are generally found in research manuscripts – such cases are, in any case, rare.

#### 15.1 *will* for making predictions

- We predict that demand **will outweigh** supply, and thus house prices **will rise**.

*will* can be used to express a hypothesis referring to one specific case, whereas the present simple is used for well-known conditions that are applicable to all cases.

- Note that if the water in the container reaches a temperature of 100C it **will boil** and this will cause damage to the samples.
- It **is well known** that if / when water **reaches** a temperature of 100C it **boils**.

*will* is rarely use to talk about future parts of the manuscript, the present simple is more common. Use present simple when outlining the structure of the article.

- This feature **is covered /** ~~**will be covered**~~ more in depth in the appendix.
- This paper is organized as follows. Section 1 **gives** ~~**/ will** give~~ a brief overview of the literature. A history of the English language **is presented** ~~**/ will be presented**~~ in Section 2.

In the Conclusions section use *will* to outline future work that you plan to carry out. However, you can reword the phrase so that you can use the present simple.

- Future work **will involve** investigating the reasons for these discrepancies.
- We **plan** to investigate the reasons for these discrepancies in a future paper.

#### 15.2 Zero and first conditional

zero conditional \[*if + present + present* or *present + if + present*\]: to express general truths, logical conclusions and scientific facts. It means ‘every time that’ or ‘whenever’.

- Papers **tend** to be rejected if the English **is** poor.
- If all humans **are** prone to corruption, then politicians **are** more prone than others.

first conditional \[*if + present + will* or *will* + *if + present*\]: to talk about real future situations, rather than general truths that are always valid.

- If **we do not receive** the revised manuscript by the end of this month, we **will be forced** to withdraw your contribution from the special issue.
- If it is true, as many authors contend, that Chinese **is set** to replace English as the international language, then this **will have** profound effects on …
- If the illness **is** in an advanced stage, then treatment **will have** little effect.

Both zero and first conditional forms can be used to express logical conclusions; *will* is sometimes preferred when there are various stages in a process and the author is talking about a later stage in the process.

- This leads to the result that if (1) **is** false then (2) **is** also false.
- The second property guarantees that if H **is** true initially, then it **will remain** true while P is being executed.

The present perfect can replace the present simple in the *if* clause. In such cases both the present simple and *will* may be found in the dependent clause.

- If this period has elapsed / elapses before x reaches y, then the system **fails / will fail.**

#### 15.3 *if* vs *when*

*if* refers to the occurrence of an event that may or may not take place, whereas *when* indicates certainty.

- **If** the alarm goes off, call the police. <small>We don’t know whether the alarm will ring or not.</small>
- I get up in the morning **when** the alarm goes off. <small>My alarm always rings every morning.</small>

#### 15.4 Second conditional

second conditional \[*if* + past simple + *would*\]: to express hypothetical situations regarding what would happen if certain features, events, scenarios were possible (which are currently not possible).

- If the government **raised** ~~**/ would raise**~~ taxes further, this **would have** serious consequences.
- **Would** the world **be** different if it **were /** ~~**would be**~~ **ruled** by women?

An alternative form of the second conditional is to replace the *if* clause with: *was* / *were* + *subject + infinitive*. This is a very formal construction and there is no real need to use it.

- **Were** citizens to pay their taxes …
- **Were** women to rule the world …

#### 15.5 Present simple versus *would*

When you report a hypothesis, i.e. how you (or another author) imagine something might be, use the present simple not *would.* Words and phrases such as *assume, assumption, hypothesize, hypothesis, suggest, argue,* and *according to* indicate to the reader that what you are saying is only a tentative proposal and not necessarily a fact.

- Aardvark’s hypothesis suggests that the onset of the disease **is /** ~~**would be**~~ **associated** with a sudden increase in blood pressure.
- Batteaux suggests that the onset of the disease **would only be associated** with a sudden increase in blood pressure if the patient **were** over the age of 50, whereas Aardvark had only hypothesized such an event for younger patients.

*would* can be used to make a statement more tentative. It is typically used with *appear, seem* and *suggest.* The present simple indicates something more certain. So your choice of *would* or present simple is based on how strong you want your statement to be.

- An intriguing hypothesis concerns the development of bilingualism. It **would seem / seems** that this can be favored when … This **would suggest / suggests** that …

#### 15.6 *would* – other uses

*would* + infinitive (not *would have* + past participle) is used in indirect speech to report what someone has said.

- The experimenter then told the students that she **would return** ~~**/ would have returned**~~ later to explain how each problem was solved.
- He said that he **would be /** ~~**would have been**~~ unable to concentrate on whatever I might have to say, because he had just seen a ghost.

*would* can be used to report a habit (a series of repeated actions) in the past particularly in reference to someone’s behavior; use of the past simple in such situations denotes that such actions may have only taken place only once rather than being repeated.

- In the first session he **showed** no strange behaviors. However at the beginning of each subsequent session he **would stare** at the wall for five minutes, then he **would turn** his head towards me and start speaking at great speed.

*would* is sometimes used to refer to how a past situation later developed in the future. In such cases the simple past could also be used, but not *will.*

- This is highlighted by Maria Kazlovic. A mentally troubled woman – she **would** ~~**/ will**~~ commit suicide two years later – she claimed that …
- His description of this species, which he would ~~**/ will**~~ revise completely in the third and final edition of his book, focused exclusively on …
- His description of this species, which he **revised** completely in the third …

#### 15.7 Third conditional

third conditional \[*if + past perfect + would have + past participle* or *would have + past participle + of + past perfect*\]: to express how things might have been if something had (not) happened.

- This mistake would not have been made if the authors **had been** ~~**/ would have been**~~ more careful.
- What **would have happened /** ~~**happened**~~ if the central bank **had not intervened** ~~**/ would not have intervened**?~~

*would have* + *past participle* can also be used in implied conditions.

- This work **would not have been** ~~**/ was not**~~ possible without the help of the following people: …
- <small>= If the following people had not helped me.</small>

*Would have* + *past participle* is not used to report what other people have said, unless what they originally said contains a third conditional.

- One juror said that she would have done exactly what the defendants had done if she **had been** ~~**/ was**~~ in their position. <small>The original words of the juror were: *I would have done exactly what the defendants did if I had been in their position.*</small>
- One migrant said that he would only **like** ~~**/ would have only liked**~~ to live in a country where everyone followed regulations and valued fairness. <small>The original words of the migrant were: *I would only like to live in a country where everyone follows regulations and values fairness.*</small>

An alternative form of the third conditional is to replace the *if* clause with: *had* + subject *+ past participle*. This is a very formal construction and there is no real need to use it.

- **Had** the physician known the true nature of the patient’s condition, she **would have never prescribed** such a high dose. <small>= If the physician had known …</small>

#### 15.8 The passive: form

Active: They *built* a new road. (past simple)

Passive: A new road *was built*. (past simple of *to be* + past participle)

Active: They *are building* a new road. (present continuous)

Passive: A new road *is being built*. (present continuous of *to be* + past participle)

#### 15.9 The passive: usage

Most books on good writing recommend using the active form to the passive form. Also applications such as Word and Grammarly often highlight any usages of the passive and suggest using the active as an alternative.

However, in research manuscripts, the passive is often a much better option. In the Methods section, for example, the main interest is NOT in who or what carried out the actions.

- The rust was removed with acid. <small>We removed the rust by acid-treatment. The rust is the key element in the phrase, not the person or thing that removed it.</small>
- An aerosol solution was added to make the flame front visible. <small>If this is in the Methods, it will be clear that you added the solution.</small>

Only use *we* or *our* in the Methods if it is not clear who carried out the action.

- In \[20\] the rust **was removed** with acid, whereas **we removed** the rust by coating it with white vinegar.

The passive is also generally preferable to the active in the following cases.

– When making general references to the literature or to what is happening in the world in general.

- Several attempts **have been made** to explain this phenomenon \[17, 2 33\]. <small>= Several researchers have attempted to …</small>
- Much progress **is being made** in the field of telecommunications.

When it is unnecessary, difficult, or impossible to identify the originator of the action.

- The surface of the steel piping **was fractured**. <small>= Something had caused the steel piping to fracture.</small>
- A large sum of money **was recently donated** to the hospital. <small>= Someone recently donated a large sum of money to the hospital.</small>

To report what is commonly believed to be true.

- This drug **is known** to have serious side effects. <small>= Serious side effects typically arise from the use of this drug.</small>

To report formal decisions or to make announcements.

- The law **is expected** to be passed next year. <small>= It is likely that the government will pass this law next year.</small>

The passive is generally used with verbs such as *install, upload* and *download.*

- The system **is installed** ~~**/ installs**~~ automatically.
- Files **are downloaded** ~~**/ download**~~ directly from source.

Note: The active equivalent might be preferential when giving oral presentations or in other more informal contexts such as emails.

#### 15.10 The active: usage

Active sentences do not necessarily have to be personal. Use the active form if this helps to shift the verb nearer to its subject. The examples in red below are NOT wrong, but they are not concise.

- Figure 4 **shows** an example of this effect.
- ~~An example of this effect **is shown** in Figure 4.~~
- The following section **outlines** the state of the art in cybertronics.
- ~~In the following section the state of the art in cybertronics **is outlined**.~~
- The system **supports**: x, y and z.
- ~~The following features **are supported** by the system: x, y and z.~~

Some passive constructions sound awkward or wrong in English, particularly with the verbs *to aim* and *to focus.* The examples in red below are NOT wrong, but are not ideal.

- **The main aim of this project is** to develop an alternative to the Internet.
- ~~**This project is mainly aimed at** developing an alternative to the Internet.~~
- This paper **focuses** on the best way to control the activities of potentially rogue traders.
- ~~This paper **is focused** on the best way to control the activities of potentially rogue traders.~~

#### 15.11 Dangerous ambiguity with the passive

An active sentence helps to the reader to understand exactly who is the agent of an action: you or another research group.

- **We found /** ~~**It was found**~~ that x is equal to y.

However, the ‘instructions to the author’ of some journals state that you should not use the personal pronoun *we*. This means that instead of writing *we did x* (active), you have to write *x was done* (passive). Unfortunately, the passive form does not tell the reader who performed the action (see 13.11).

In the red sentence below, the readers first have to check the references/bibliography to see who the author of 34 is. Then they have to recheck who the authors of the present paper are in order to see if they are the same authors as those of 34.

- ~~It **was found** that children perform such tasks better than adults \[34\].~~

The first blue sentence below makes it much clearer for the reader by providing the name of the author within the main sentence. The second blue sentence uses *we*, so the reader knows that it is you and not some other author,

- Peters **found** that children perform such tasks better than adults \[34\].
- In a previous paper \[34\], **we found** that children perform such tasks better than adults.

If you are talking about the literature and you use the passive both to refer to your own work and that in the literature, then the reader will have difficulty distinguishing between the two. Although some journals dislike *we*, they don’t seem to have problems with *our*! So one good way to avoid possible misunderstanding is to use expressions such as *our results show, in our work, in our study*. Using such expressions is vital when you are constantly switching from talking about the literature to talking about your work.

- These features are generally characteristic of this species \[Smith 2023, Carsten 2024\]. However, **in our study, it was found** that they are also characteristic of some completely unrelated species.
- ~~These features are generally characteristic of this species \[Smith 2023, Carsten 2024\]. However, **it was found** that they are also characteristic of some completely unrelated species.~~

Be careful when using expressions such as *in a previous work* – it must be clear that you are talking about your own previous work, rather than the previous work of an author you have just mentioned (13.11).

- ~~Ying et al. noted that red is most people’s favorite color. However, **in a previous work** it was noted that green was …~~ <small>Not clear whose previous work is being referred to.</small>
- Ying et al. noted that red is most people’s favorite color. However, **in a previous work carried out by our group**, it was noted that green was …

If you use the passive to talk about something which is commonly referred to in the literature, then it will help the reader if you use a word or expression that indicates that this is common knowledge.

- ~~Children are conditioned by their parents \[7, 9\]. Thus it **is assumed** that children in orphanages will …~~ <small>It is impossible to understand who has made or is making the assumption.</small>
- Children are conditioned by their parents \[7, 9\]. Thus it **is generally assumed** that children in orphanages will …<small>*generally* indicates that this is an assumption made in the literature and not specifically by the authors of this paper</small>
- Children are conditioned by their parents \[7, 9\]. It **is well known** that children who have been abandoned by their parents will … <small>*it is well known* clarifies that this is not just the author’s viewpoint.</small>
