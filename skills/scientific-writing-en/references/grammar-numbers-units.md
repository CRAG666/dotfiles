## Source B

### 4 Grammar

At present, computer grammar checkers can offer only simplified, mechanical advice, and following that advice without reference to context can in some cases lead to worse results. For example, the most common word processor often underlines the passive indiscriminately, even when it is the most appropriate form.

Therefore, although a computer can offer some *guidance*, this must be treated with caution – even poetry by Shakespeare and prose by Jane Austen are marked as incorrect by current software. Where appropriate, however, this book gives tips on using your computer to check your grammar.

### 4.1 Noncount nouns

Some words common in university and research writing do not normally have a plural form, and many cannot be used with *a* or *an*. These are called noncount nouns and a number of examples are given below:

#### 4.1.1 education

##### Incorrect

- educations

##### Examples of correct use

- a university education
- Education is very important.
- Most universities offer a wide range of courses (**not** educations).

#### 4.1.2 equipment

##### Incorrect

- an equipment
- equipments

##### Examples of correct use

- a piece of equipment
- The equipment was faulty.
- Some of the equipment was damaged.
- Much of the equipment was out of date.

#### 4.1.3 evidence

##### Incorrect

- an evidence

##### Examples of correct use

- The evidence points to …
- There is some evidence to suggest that …
- There is a great deal of evidence for …
- There is no evidence of …

#### 4.1.4 information

##### Incorrect

- an information
- informations

##### Examples of correct use

- an important piece of information
- some information
- Much of the information was incorrect.
- We have no information on …

#### 4.1.5 knowledge

##### Incorrect

- knowledges

##### Examples of correct use

- A knowledge of statistics is essential.
- The author demonstrates an intimate knowledge of …
- We now have a great deal of knowledge of …
- We have little knowledge of …

#### 4.1.6 progress

##### Incorrect

- a progress
- progresses

##### Examples of correct use

- We have made limited progress.
- We have made some progress.
- We have made a great deal of progress towards …
- We have made no progress.

#### 4.1.7 research

##### Incorrect

- a research
- researches

##### Examples of correct use

- an important piece of research
- Further research is necessary …
- Most of our research was carried out …
- A great deal of research is concerned with …

#### 4.1.8 software

##### Incorrect

- a software
- softwares

##### Examples of correct use

- a computer program
- We used the following software:

### 4.2 Singular words ending in *-s*

#### 4.2.1 Subjects and diseases

Some names of subjects and diseases, which appear to be plural because they end in *-s*, are normally used as singular. These include:

- acoustics
- aeronautics
- dynamics
- economics
- electronics
- ergonomics
- ethics
- forensics
- genetics
- hydraulics
- linguistics
- mathematics
- obstetrics
- optics
- paediatrics GB or pediatrics US
- phonetics
- physics
- politics
- robotics
- semantics
- semiotics
- statistics
- tectonics

And

- diabetes
- measles
- mumps
- rabies
- rickets
- scabies
- shingles

Also

- news

##### Examples

- Economics is taught at many universities.
- Linguistics is the study of language.
- Obstetrics is a branch of medicine.
- Politics is a dirty business.
- Diabetes is a growing problem.
- The news about Dolly was a great surprise.

##### Exceptions

There are some exceptions when the word denotes not the name but rather the application of the subject. It may then be used as plural, for example:

- Good acoustics are important.
- Economics dictate many aspects of our lives.
- His politics are well known.
- These statistics are unreliable.

And a form without *-s* can occur in some words:

- A strong work ethic
- One statistic worth noting is that …
- A common tactic

### 4.3 Irregular plurals

Some words borrowed from other languages, in particular Latin, Greek and French, have irregular plurals in English. Although many now have the usual English *-s* plural, some still retain a Latin, Greek or French plural ending, while others have both. For example, the plural of *stimulus* is *stimuli*, the plural of *focus* is *focuses* or *foci*, and the plural of *virus* is *viruses*.

Your computer spelling checker will alert you to incorrect plural forms, though in some cases preference for the original or the English *-s* plural differs from subject to subject and between formal and informal language.

### 4.4 Confusing words: singular and plural

#### 4.4.1 data

Although *data* is the plural of Latin *datum*, it is commonly used in English as singular.

In formal academic language, however, *data* is still often plural:

- The data are inconclusive.

#### 4.4.2 criteria

*Criteria* is the plural of *criterion*:

##### Singular

- The sole criterion in the selection process was …

##### Plural

- Many criteria were used in the selection process.

#### 4.4.3 phenomena

*Phenomena* is the plural of *phenomenon*:

##### Singular

- This phenomenon can be seen in …

##### Plural

- Several different phenomena can be observed …

#### 4.4.4 media

*Media* is the plural of *medium*:

##### Singular

- The internet is a new and exciting medium.

##### Plural

- The media are often blamed for society’s ills.

#### 4.4.5 bacteria

*Bacteria* is the plural of *bacterium*:

##### Singular

- A single bacterium is too small to be seen with the naked eye.

##### Plural

- While some bacteria are harmful, others are beneficial.

#### 4.4.6 strata

*Strata* is the plural of *stratum*:

##### Singular

- Each stratum represents a different period.

##### Plural

- Sequences of sedimentary strata

##### Writing practice

1. Following the sections above, check your text for singular and plural forms
   - a. Did you find any errors?
2. Using the confusing words section above, construct new sentences with each of the words, using the singular and plural forms correctly
3. In class, in groups or in pairs, exchange texts and evaluate each other’s writing, going through the points above

### 4.5 Confusing words: pronouns

#### 4.5.1 it’s – its

*It’s* with an apostrophe is short for *it is*. The short form is not used in formal writing:

- It’s raining.

*Its* without an apostrophe is a possessive pronoun:

- The Academy will hold its next meeting in Boston.

#### 4.5.2 who’s – whose

*Who’s* is short for *who is* and is not used in formal writing:

- Who’s responsible for this?

*Whose* means *of whom* or *of which*:

- Novak, whose recent paper on …

#### 4.5.3 you’re – your

*You’re* is short for *you are* and is not used in formal writing:

- You’re wrong!

*Your* is a possessive pronoun:

- Your paper has been accepted for publication.

#### 4.5.4 we’re – were – where

*We’re* is short for *we are* and is not used in formal writing:

- We’re hungry!

*Were* is the plural of *was*:

- We were very surprised at the results: they were completely unexpected.

*Were* is also used with *you*, whether singular or plural:

- You were right, Professor Clever Clogs.
- Professor Clever Clogs and Dr Smarty Pants, you were both wrong.

*Where* means *at which place*:

- Where are you giving your paper?

#### 4.5.5 they’re – their – there

*They’re* is short for *they are* and is not used in formal writing:

- They’re off!

*Their* is a possessive pronoun:

- Watson and Crick made their breakthrough in Cambridge.

*There* means *that place*:

- We should go there one day.

*There* is also used in *there is* and *there are*:

- There are several possible explanations for …

##### Computer help

To check for confusing words and contracted forms in Microsoft Word, go to Options – Spelling & Grammar – Settings or Word Options – Proofing – Grammar Settings and check the ‘Commonly confused words’ or ‘Misused words’ and ‘Contractions’ boxes (these options may depend on your program version).

#### 6.1.1 Words or figures?

A common rule for writing numbers is to write *one*, *two*, *three*, *four*, *five*, *six*, *seven*, *eight*, *nine* and *ten* as words, but larger numbers as figures.

The following numbers may also be written as words in normal text:

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
| twenty | sixty | hundred |
| thirty | seventy | thousand |
| forty | eighty | million |
| fifty | ninety | billion |

##### Approximate versus exact

Compare also the examples below where words are used for approximate amounts and figures for exact values:

- There have been over fifty new cases this year.
- There have been 54 new cases this year.
- There are around five thousand new students every year.
- Last year there were 5023 new students.

##### Use figures with units or abbreviations

|  |  |
|---|---|
| 3% | 10 kg |
| 6 mm | 100 km |
| 25 °C | pH 7 |

##### Use figures with page, figure and table numbers

|  |  |
|---|---|
| page 1 | p. 100 |
| Figure 6 | Fig. 8 |
| Table 3 |  |

##### Use figures with dates

- 19 February 2008
- October 11, 2009

#### 6.1.2 Avoid beginning a sentence with a figure

In university and research writing you should avoid beginning a sentence with a figure, for example:

- 1 in 10 pregnancies ...
- **Better:** One in ten pregnancies …
- 2.5 mg of distilled water were added after 30 minutes …
- **Better:** After 30 minutes, 2.5 mg of distilled water were added …
- 50% of students at some UK universities come from private schools …
- **Better:** Fifty percent of students at some UK universities come from private schools …

#### 6.1.3 Avoid mixing words and figures

Where possible, avoid mixing words and figures in the same sentence, particularly when comparing. For example:

- Compulsory education in Britain is from five to 16, though many children begin preschool at 3 or four.
- **Better:** Compulsory education in Britain is from 5 to 16, though many children begin preschool at 3 or 4.

##### Avoiding ambiguity

However, where necessary numbers should be written as words or figures to avoid ambiguity, for example:

- three 5-point scales
- five 10-year-old children

##### Values over a million

Some values over a million, particularly currencies, may be written as a combination of figures and words, particularly when round figures:

- £3 million
- USD 10 billion

Similarly with populations:

- The population of the UK is around 60 million.

#### 6.1.4 Hundreds

In English, do not add -*s* to specific multiples of a *hundred*, *thousand*, *million* or *billion*. For example:

- two hundred (**not** two hundreds)
- three thousand (**not** three thousands)
- four million (**not** four millions)
- five billion (**not** five billions)

However, an *-s* is indeed added to the general multiples *hundreds*, *thousands*, *millions* and *billions*:

- Hundreds of researchers attended this year’s conference.
- Thousands of people die of preventable diseases every day.
- The cost may run into the millions, even billions.

In British English, when pronouncing numbers over 100 or writing them in words, *and* is always added before any units under 100:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| 110 | one hundred **and** ten |
| 125 | one hundred **and** twenty-five |
| 150 | one hundred **and** fifty |
| 175 | one hundred **and** seventy-five |

#### 6.1.5 Thousands

Multiples of a thousand are marked by a space or comma. A common rule in university and research texts is to write numbers under 10 000 without commas or spacing, and numbers 10 000 and over with commas or spacing. Some authors and publishers also divide four-figure numbers, for example 3 987 or 3,987.

| **Spacing** | **Commas** |
|---|---|
| 3987 or 3 987 | 3987 or 3,987 |
| 29 483 | 29,483 |
| 6 728 349 | 6,728,349 |

A space is less ambiguous as the comma is used in some countries as the decimal symbol, for instance 3,142. As always, be consistent throughout your text and follow any guidelines for your subject or publication.

##### Do not use a point to mark thousands

In English, unlike some languages, the point is not used to mark thousands:

- 3.500 means 3½, **not** 3500

##### Do not add commas or spaces in page numbers

- page 1234, **not** page 1 234 or page 1,234

##### Computer help

To prevent spaced numbers from being split at the end of a line in Microsoft Word, use a nonbreaking space: Ctrl + Shift + space (this option may depend on your program version).

#### 6.1.6 Billion

Beware of ambiguity when reading or writing *billion* in British English:

- £5 billion
- £10 billion

Although *billion* is now normally used in British English to mean a thousand million (10<sup>9</sup>) as in US English, it also has an earlier meaning of a million million (10<sup>12</sup>).

#### 6.1.7 Decimals

Decimals in English are indicated by a point, not a comma as in some languages. For example:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| a half | 0.5 |
| a third | 0.333 |
| a quarter or fourth | 0.25 |
| a fifth | 0.2 |

If you normally use a comma for decimals in your country, be sure to carefully check your figures in English as this is a potential source of major error.

#### 6.1.8 Fractions

Common, single fractions do not need a hyphen:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| ½ | one half |
| ⅓ | one third |
| ¼ | one quarter or fourth |
| ⅛ | one eighth |

But others generally do:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| ⅔ | two-thirds |
| ¾ | three-quarters or three-fourths |
| ⅜ | three-eighths |
| ⅝ | five-eighths |
| ⅞ | seven-eighths |

#### 6.1.9 Percentages

The British spelling is *per cent* as two words and the US spelling *percent* as one word, though this is not a hard-and-fast distinction.

You should be consistent in your text in your use of *%*, *per cent* or *percent*:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| ten per cent | ten percent |
| 10 per cent | 10 percent |
| 10% | **not** ten % |

The % symbol should always be written next to its number without a space:

- 100%
- **Not:** 100 %

#### 6.1.10 Mathematical symbols

Be consistent in the spacing of symbols in your text, for example:

- 1 + 1 = 2
- 5 − 3 = 2

#### 7.2 Words vs numerals: basic rules

In this section I use *number* or *numeral* to mean 1, 2, 67, 765 etc and *word* to mean one, two, sixty seven etc.

When you use numbers from one to ten (or nine, depending on the journal) within a written text, use words not numerals. This helps the number to stand out visually. For example, *1* is harder to see than *one*.

- For the color measurements, **three** ~~**/ 3**~~ fruits of each cultivar were analyzed.

At the beginning of a sentence, use the word (e.g. *eleven*) rather than the numeral (e.g. 11). If necessary, rearrange the sentence so that the numeral does not appear at the beginning.

- **Two hundred /** ~~**202**~~ samples were examined.
- ~~**50%** of users do not use this feature.~~
- This feature is not used by **50%** of users.
- **An amount of 1.85 mL /** ~~**1.85 mL**~~ of distilled water was added to the mixture.
- **Seventy per cent /** ~~**70%**~~ of managers believe that praising employees makes no difference to performance.
- **Two thirds /** ~~**2/3**~~ of those interviewed said that **one fifth** of their income was spent on fuel.

Do not mix words and numerals within the same context.

- ~~There were **202 thousand** people at the conference.~~
- There were **202,000** people at the conference.
- ~~There were **two- to 3-fold** increases.~~
- There were **two- to three**-fold increases.
- ~~Of the 270 examined fecal samples, **46** were positive for Trichuridae eggs: **six** (2.2%) were positive for *E. boehmi* (Fig. 1a)*,* **twelve** (4.4%) *E. aerophilus* (Fig. 1b) and **thirty-three** (12.2%) for *T. vulpis* (Fig. 1c).~~
- Of the 270 examined fecal samples, 46 were positive for Trichuridae eggs: **6** (2.2%) were positive for *E. boehmi* (Fig. 1a)*,* **12** (4.4%) *E. aerophilus* (Fig. 1b) and **33** (12.2%) for *T. vulpis* (Fig. 1c).

In the last example above numerals are used rather than words for all the numbers (including six). This break the rule of not using a numeral for numbers lower than ten, but the overriding rule is consistency, i.e. all numbers are written in the same way in the same sentence.

In the case below, it makes sense to use a mix of numerals and words to avoid confusion for the reader.

- ~~In Tables **1 and 2, 20** samples with ...~~
- In **Tables 1 and 2, twenty** samples with ...
- In **Tables 1 and 2, a total of 20** samples with ...

Times of day are written as numerals; use the 24 hour clock to avoid having to use *a.m.* (before midday) or *p.m.* (after midday).

- ~~Rats were fed at **9 o’clock** in the morning and at **5 p.m.** every day.~~
- Rats were fed at **09.00** and **17.00** every day.

#### 7.3 When 1–10 can be used as numerals rather than words

Use numerals not words when numbers are in association with percentages, abbreviations for measurements, tables, and figures etc.

- As shown in **Table 3,** the patient was only **1.20 m** tall and weighed **9 kg**. Her percentage body fat was **9.9%.**

You can optionally use numerals rather than words when:

The second number in a range of numbers is higher than eleven; alternatively write both numbers as words.

- The process usually takes between **4 and 12** days.
- The process usually takes between **four and twelve** days.

There is a series of numbers, or in ratios and proportions.

- In the last three years the numbers have risen by **11, 6 and 7**, respectively.
- In the last three years the numbers have risen by **eleven, six and seven**, respectively.

#### 7.4 Making numbers plural

Whole numbers do not require an *-s* to indicate the plural. *of* is not used between the number and the noun.

- **Four thousand /** ~~**Four thousands of**~~ experiments have been conducted so far.
- In the **1990s**, many airlines flew Boeing **747s.**

An exception to the above rule is in expressions such as *tens, dozens, hundreds, thousands*, i.e. to indicate large generic numbers. In such cases *of* follows the number.

- **Hundreds of** people were at the conference, and **thousands** were online.

Apostrophes are not required.

- ~~In the **1990’s**, many airlines flew Boeing **747’s.**~~

Fractions require an *-s* plural.

- One and a half **hours** (= an hour and a half), three **quarters** of an hour, four **fifths** of a liter, nine **tenths** of a second.

A noun which follows a number is used in the singular form when acting as an adjective. Note the use of hyphens.

- ~~A 51 **years** old patient~~
- A 51-**year**-old patient
- A multi-**megabyte** ~~**/ megabytes**~~ memory
- a **3-point** turn, a **4-day** week, a **size 7** component, a **6-year-old** child
- a **six-year-old** child

#### 7.5 Singular or plural verbs with numbers

Numbers and quantities require the verb that follows to be in the singular form. This is because they are seen as a mass rather than individual items.

- Two weeks **is** ~~**/ are**~~ not enough.
- Three hundred kilometers **is** ~~**/ are**~~ not too far.
- Clearly, $5,000,000 **is** ~~**/ are**~~ a lot of money.

The use of *there is / was* and *there are / were* depends on whether the noun that follows is in the singular or plural, respectively.

- In this diagram there **is** ~~**/ are**~~ **a** rectangle and two squares.
- In this diagram there **are two** squares and a rectangle.

Use *another*, not *other*, before a number.

- We need to do **another** ~~**/ other**~~ three tests. <small>= We need to do **three other** tests.</small>

*None* is followed by a verb in the plural.

- None of the tests **give** ~~**/ gives**~~ optimum results.
- None of them **give** ~~**/ gives**~~ optimum results.

#### 7.6 Decimals and whole numbers

Decimals are not written as words and are written with a point (.) rather than a comma (,).

- ~~The student scored **two point four** and **two point six** in the first two tests.~~
- ~~The student scored **0,4** and **6** in the first two tests.~~
- The student scored **2.4** and **2.6** in the first two tests.
- The student scored **0.4** and **1.6** in the first two tests.

Commas tend to be used in whole numbers above 999 (but not in dates).

- The faculty has a total of **2,563** students.
- The faculty has a total of **24,563** ~~**/ 24563**~~ students.
- The Battle of Hastings was in **1066 /** ~~**1,066**~~**.**

#### 7.7 Abbreviations, symbols, percentages, fractions, and ordinals

Always use numerals, rather than words, with abbreviations or symbols.

- $2000 / two thousand dollars ~~/ $two thousand~~
- 68c / sixty-eight cents ~~/ sixty-eight c~~

Do not combine spelled forms of numbers with symbols.

- **Two thirds** of those interviewed said that ~~**1/5**~~ of their income was spent on fuel.

*Percentage* is one word, both *percent* (one word) and *per cent* (two words) are correct. Do not use *%age.*

- The **percentage** ~~**/ %age**~~ of students who ...

In ranges of percentages, the percentage symbol is located after the second number or after both numbers.

- The disease is fatal in **2%–3% / 2–3%** ~~**/ 2%–3**~~ of cases.

Symbols generally come after the number. Exceptions: currencies (e.g. ¥100, €56).

- The total cost was **$5000** ~~**/ 5000$**~~
- The total cost was **5000 USD / US dollars**

Before abbreviations and symbols use numerals (e.g. 7) rather than words (e.g. *seven*).

- ~~The patient weighed **sixty five kg**.~~
- The patient weighed **65kg / 65 kg**.

Most abbreviations for measurements are all lower case. Exceptions: bytes (e.g. GB, KB); micro measurements (mL – microliter, milliliter); and temperatures (C, F).

Do not use an abbreviation for a measurement without a number.

- ~~A few **μg** of (e.g. **3μg**) ...~~
- A few **micrograms** (e.g. **3μg**) ...

#### 7.8 Abbreviations and symbols in the humanities

The humanities and social sciences tend to use words rather than abbreviations and symbols.

- ~~It took King Harold’s men **10 d** to cover the **400 km** from York to at the Battle of Hastings in temperatures ranging from −**20°C to 3°C**.~~
- It took King Harold’s men **ten days** to cover the **four hundred kilometers** from York to fight at the Battle of Hastings, in temperatures that ranged from **twenty degrees below zero to three degrees above**.

#### 7.9 Measurements: adjectives, nouns and verbs

Measurements using nouns can normally be expressed in several ways, either with the verb *to have* or the verb *to be*.

When the measurement appears after the noun, then use *of* as the preposition (*a width of 2 cm*); if it appears before the noun use *in* (*2 cm in width*).

- The **length of** the field **was** 202 meters.
- The field **had** a **length of** 202 meters.
- The field **was** 202 meters **in length**.
- These cores **were** approximately **1.5 mm in diameter** and **25 mm in height**.
- These cores **had** an approximate **diameter of 1.5 mm** and a **height of 25 mm**.
- The girl **was** 120 cm **tall**.
- The girl **had** a **height** of 120 cm.

When measurements appear in brackets they are often not written as full sentences.

- Samples were individually stored in fresh glass vials (**diameter: 1 cm; length: 6 cm**) until the testing phase.

#### 7.10 Comparatives and superlatives: -*er*, -*est*, irregular forms

All monosyllable adjectives require *-er / -est* (exceptions: *more true* or *truer*, *more real*). All adjectives with three or more syllables require *more / most.*

- ~~This is the **most big** and **productivest** machine in the world.~~
- This is the **biggest** and **most productive** machine in the world.

Two-syllable adjectives ending in a vowel sound (e.g. *easy*, *happy, narrow*) take *-er / -est*, whereas those ending in a consonant sound (e.g. *complex, massive, useful*) require *more / most.* Note: *clever*, *common, friendly, gentle, quiet* and *simple* and be used with either form *(most common, commonest).*

- ~~This is the **most busy** and **most heavy** period of the year, but yet also the **peacefullest**.~~
- This is the **busiest** and **heaviest** period of the year, but yet also the **most peaceful**.

Use the comparative form (e.g. *bigger, better, more beautiful*) to compare two things or two groups of things.

- Brazil is **bigger** than Argentina.
- Brazil is **biggest** than Argentina.
- The system performed **better / worse / less efficiently / more efficiently** in the first test than in the second test.
- The system performed **best / worst / least efficiently / most efficiently** in the first test than in the second test.

Use the superlative form (e.g. the *biggest, the best, the most beautiful*) to describe something in absolute terms.

- The application returns only the **most** ~~**/ more**~~ relevant results.
- It always chooses the **best** ~~**/ better**~~ solution.
- Mumbai and Sao Paulo are big cities, but Tokyo is the **biggest** ~~**/ bigger**~~ and **most** ~~**/ more**~~ **populated** in the world.

Note that *the* is generally used before all superlatives.

- ~~This candidate was certainly **best / the better**.~~
- This candidate was certainly **the best**.

Note these irregular forms: *good / better / best; bad / worse / worst; far / further / furthest* (alternative spelling: *farther / farthest*).

- They traveled **further** ~~**/ farer**~~ than the others.

#### 7.11 Comparisons of (in)equality

Use *than* when comparing two or more items. Avoid unnecessary use of *with respect to / in comparison to / compared to.*

- ~~China is **bigger of** the United States.~~
- ~~China is **big with respect to** the USA.~~
- China is bigger **than** the United States.

To say that items are the same, use *the same as.*

- Australia is approximately **the same** size **as** ~~**than / of**~~ the 48 mainland states of the USA.

When indicating that two things are equal in terms of a particular quality, use *as ... as.*

- This book is **as** good **as /** ~~**than**~~ that book.

When making negative comparisons *less* tends to be used only with multi- syllable adjectives. Use *not as ... as* with monosyllables or multisyllables.

- ~~This solution is **less good than** the other one.~~
- This solution **is not as good as** the other one.
- ~~The first is not so good **like** the second.~~
- The first is not as good **as** the second.
- ~~This solution is less efficient **as** the other one.~~
- This solution is **not as efficient as** the other one.
- This solution is **less efficient than** the other one.

#### 7.12 Ranges of values and use of hyphens

You can introduce a range of values in three different ways:

- There should be **11**–**20** participants.
- There should be **from 11 to 20** participants.
- There should be **between 11 and 20** participants.

Use a hyphen to indicate a range of values with numerals. But to indicate a range of values with words, use *to.*

- The courses last **15**–**20** weeks / **15 to 20** weeks ~~**/ fifteen–twenty weeks**~~.
- The course will last **3-4** weeks / **three to four** weeks ~~**/ three–four** weeks~~.

#### 7.13 *once, twice* vs *one time, two times*

*Once* = one time, *twice* = two times. *once* and *twice* are more commonly found than *one time* and *two times* – avoid mixing the two forms in the same phrase.

- ~~The tests should be repeated at least **twice or three times**.~~
- The tests should be repeated at least **two or three times**.

*Once* and *twice* cannot be used after expressions such as a *minimum / maximum of.*

- ~~The test should repeated a **minimum of twice**.~~
- The test should repeated a **minimum of two times**.

*thrice* is antiquated.

- The tests should be repeated **three times** ~~**/ thrice**~~.

#### 7.14 Ordinal numbers, -*nd*, -*rd*, -*th*, Latin numbers

*2nd, 3rd, 4th* etc tend to be used above all in the names of conferences. Note: There is no space between the numeral and the letters.

- The **14th** ~~**/ 14 th**~~ Annual European Data Economy Conference.
- The **33rd** ~~**/ 33 rd**~~ Health Data Science Symposium.

Do not use 2nd, 3rd, 4th etc in the middle of a sentence, unless referring to a conference etc.

- This was the **second** ~~**/ 2nd**~~ time this had happened.

Avoid *2nd, 3rd, 4th* in dates because you might make mistakes when using them.

- From **3 March / March 3rd / 3th March** to **13 April / 13th April** ~~**/ 13rd April**~~.

Roman numerals are used with names of people.

- John Paul Getty **III**, King Charles **III,** and Pope John Paul **II** never met all together, but if they had ...
- ~~John Paul Getty **3rd**, King Charles **3rd,** and Pope John Paul **2nd** never met all together, but if they had ...~~

Roman numerals are not used with centuries (7.15).

- ~~They can be dated to a time-span ranging from the **VII century BC** to the **II century AD**.~~
- They can be dated to a time-span ranging from the **7th century BCE** to the **2nd century CE**.

Arabic numerals are used much more frequently than Roman numerals (I, II, III) for section numbering in papers.

- This is dealt with in more detail in **Sections 3** and **4** ~~**/ Sections III and IV**~~.

#### 7.15 Dates

The world has three principal systems for writing dates: Form A: (dmy) 10 March 2020=10.03.2020. Form B: (mdy) March 10, 2020=03.10.2020. Form C: (ymd) 2020 March 10=2020-10-03.

The first form (number month year) is perhaps the clearest. To avoid confusion, always write the date with the month as a word.

- ~~They calculate that the world will end on **03.10.2030.**~~

In the US, the above sentence would be interpreted as 10 October not 10 March. In most of the rest of the world it would be interpreted as 10 March. So it is best to write as follows:

- Smith et al. calculate that the world will end on **10 March 2030**.

Write centuries with Arabic numerals not Latin numerals.

Prefer non- religious alternatives to BC (before Christ) and AD (anno domini – year of our Lord). You can use BCE (before common era) and CE (common era), and also BPE (before present era) and PE (present era).

- ~~They can be dated to a time-span ranging from the **VII century** BCE to the **II century** CE.~~
- They can be dated to a time-span ranging from the **7th century** BCE to the **2nd century** CE.

Write decades in their full numerical form (1980s), rather than abbreviated form (’80s) - otherwise there could be confusion between centuries. Also use the plural *s* without an apostrophe.

- ~~This paper presents an analysis of the music of the **’90s / 1990’s**.~~
- This paper presents an analysis of the techno-rhythms of the music of the **1990**s.

### 9 Nouns: countable vs uncountable, plurals

#### 9.1 Countable nouns: use with articles

A countable noun is something you can count.

- 30 books, many manuscripts, 100 apples, several PCs.

Before a singular countable noun you must put an article (*a / an* or *the*). For exceptions see 9.2.

- **A book** ~~**/ Book**~~ is still an excellent source of information.
- **The book** that I am reading is about …
- This acts as **an alternative** ~~**/** as **alternative.**~~
- When I was **a student** ~~**/** was **student**.~~
- You cannot leave **the country** ~~**/** leave **country**~~ without **a passport** ~~**/** without **passport**.~~

If you are talking about something in general, then do not use *the* with plural nouns.

- **Funds** ~~**/ The funds**~~ are essential for research.
- Throughout the world, **full professors** ~~**/ the full professors**~~ tend to earn more than **researchers** ~~**/ the researchers**~~.

Scientific*/*technical acronyms whose last letter stands for a countable noun behave like other countable nouns. They thus require an article when used in the singular, and an *-s* when used in the plural.

- Access requires **a PIN** (personal identification number).
- The number of purchases of **CDs** is only 0.1% of what it was 35 years ago.

After *as* and *in*, a few singular countable nouns are often (but not always) used without the article.

- We used a 5-kR resistor placed **in series**.
- All non-dummy variables are **in log form.**
- We used X as input, and Y **as output**.

#### 9.2 Singular countable nouns: with and without *a / an*

In scientific English, with certain terms - *under license, at x temperature, without loss of* - no article is required when talking in general. However, when adding specific details, the article is required (the second in the pairs of examples below).

- The software is used under **license** from IBM.
- We obtained **a license** to use the software.
- The samples were stored at **room temperature.**
- The guinea-pigs were housed singly or in pairs at **a room temperature** of 20–22°C.
- We examined the reaction between methylchloride and chloride ion in the gas phase and in **aqueous solution** using techniques based on …
- This was followed by etching in **an aqueous solution** of phosphoric acid and chromic acid.

When preceded with *by*, means of transport are used without *a / an*; certain time expressions do not require *a / an* when used with prepositions.

- They rented **a car** to travel through India.
- They traveled through India **by car**. They drove **by night**. They discovered that it often rains in India **in \[the\] summer**.

#### 9.3 Uncountable nouns: general rules

An uncountable noun is seen as a mass, rather than as several clearly separate parts. For example *traffic* is a mass – we are not interested in the exact number of cars, they all seem to be merged together in the distance. But if I say ‘look at those three *cars’*, you can clearly differentiate between one car and another. Thus *traffic* is uncountable, and *cars* are countable.

Uncountable nouns cannot be made plural, i.e. you cannot put an ‘s’ at the end of the word. This means that they are not used with plural forms (e.g. *are, have; these, those; few, many*).

- **This information is /** ~~**These informations are**~~ confidential.
- **Feedback /** ~~**Feedbacks**~~ from users on usage of the software **has /** ~~**have**~~ shown that …
- The **news is /** ~~**are**~~ good – our manuscript has been accepted.

Uncountable nouns cannot be preceded with words such as: *a, an, one, many, few, several, these* (i.e. words that in some way indicate that a distinct number of items is involved).

- Our institute only has **a little money** ~~**/ a few money**~~ available for funding.
- We have not done **much research** ~~**/ many researches / one research**~~ in this area.
- Written **consent was /** ~~**consents were**~~ obtained from all patients.
- She has **expertise /** ~~**an expertise**~~ in this field.

There are hundreds of uncountable nouns, some examples frequently used in research are: *access, accommodation, advertising, advice\*, agriculture* (and other subjects of study), *capital, cancer* (and other diseases and illnesses), *consent, electricity* (and other intangibles), *English* (and other languages), *equipment\*, evidence\*, expertise, feedback, functionality, furniture\*, gold\** (and other metals), *hardware, health, industry, inflation, information\*, intelligence, luck, knowhow, luggage\*, machinery\*, money, news, oxygen* (and other gases), *personnel, poverty, progress, research, safety, security, software, staff, storage, traffic, training, transport, waste, wealth, welfare, wildlife.*

The uncountable nouns listed above with an asterisk (\*) can be used with *a piece/item of*.

- They gave me **a piece of advice** ~~**/ an advice.**~~
- We have two **pieces of equipment** ~~**/** two **equipments**.~~
- We need **several new pieces of equipment** and **some software**.
- We need ~~**several new equipments**~~ and ~~**a software**~~**.**

#### 9.4 Uncountable nouns: specific rules

Some nouns are both countable and uncountable, but with a difference in meaning.

- **Paper** and **coffee** are becoming expensive commodities.
- She has **a coffee** <small>(i.e. a cup of coffee)</small> and reads **a paper** <small>(i.e. a newspaper)</small> every day.
- She has just finished **another paper.** <small>(i.e. a manuscript)</small>
- The role of traditional **medicine** is being undermined by alternative **medicine**.
- The occurrence and fate of **medicines** in the environment – i.e. how they are absorbed into the water and soil systems – has rarely been investigated.
- The explosion caused considerable **damage** to the machine.
- The company has been awarded **damages** <small>(i.e. compensation)</small> as a result of the lawsuit.
- This **work** <small>(i.e. this research, manuscript)</small> is worth publishing.
- The field of the cultural heritage investigates ways of preserving **works** of art <small>(e.g. buildings, paintings, sculptures).</small>

Some nouns are used in both their singular and plural forms, with no difference in meaning. However, everyday English tends to prefer the singular form.

- Teenagers often exhibit **behavior / behaviors** that is annoying for adults.

To express the plural of certain uncountable words, sometimes you need to choose another word.

- She has ~~**expertises**~~ in many areas.
- She is **an expert** in …
- The ~~**functionalities**~~ of this application are outstanding.
- The **features** of this …
- I have done ~~**several works**~~ both in industry and research.
- I have done several **jobs** …
- We need ~~**a software**~~ to do this**.**
- We need **a program** */* **an app / a software application / some software** …
- We have ~~**a training**~~ tomorrow.
- We have **a training course / seminar / session** tomorrow.

#### 9.5 Regular plurals

To form the plural of most countable nouns (including acronyms), simply add *s* or *es* to the end of the word.

- We tested the engines of three car**s**, two taxi**s**, six lorr**ies**, and four bus**es**.
- Four **PCs** ~~**/ PC**~~**.**

In a *noun + of + noun* construction where the two nouns indicate a single entity, the first noun is made plural.

- Several **points of view** ~~**/ point of views**~~ have been put forward in the literature.

Adjectives are never made plural.

- We also analyzed three **other** ~~**/ others**~~ papers on this topic.

Nouns that act as adjectives are not made plural. In the example below, *car* acts as an adjective describing which type of *production* and *sales*.

- **Car** ~~**/ Cars**~~ production is rising, but **car** ~~**/ cars**~~ sales are falling.
- <small>= The production of cars is rising but the sales of cars are falling.</small>

A noun which follows a number (or an implied number) is used in the singular form when acting as an adjective. Note the use of hyphens (1.14).

- I have a 24**-year**-old ~~/ 24**-years**-old~~ student helping me in the lab.
- <small>= The student is 24 **years** old.</small>
- This work is part of a three-**phase** ~~**/ phases**~~ study into psychotic behavior amongst TEFL teachers.
- This work is part of a three- study into psychotic behavior amongst TEFL teachers.

*-fold* is a suffix to indicate a specified number of parts or times. It does not have a plural *-s.* Again, note the use of hyphens (1.14).

- The increase was **3-fold** ~~**/ folds**~~.
- <small>= There was a **3-fold** increase.</small>

#### 9.6 Irregular plurals

Some irregular plurals: *child / children, man / men, woman / women, half / halves, knife / knives, life / lives, foot / feet, tooth / teeth*.

- The patients consisted of three **children**, four adult **men**, and six adult **women**, all with persistent problems with their **teeth**.

*Fish* and *sheep* are not normally made plural. The plural of *mouse* (the animal) is *mice*, for the computer device the plural is *mouses*.

- This paper compares the relative brain powers of **fish** ~~**/ fishes**~~ and **sheep** ~~**/ sheeps**.~~
- All subjects were provided with PCs, monitors, headphones and **mouses** ~~**/ mice**.~~

*Data* can be followed by the singular or plural – the plural form is more common in science. The singular form of data is *datum*, but *data* is more commonly used in both the singular and plural.

- **This data is** ***/*** **These data are** inconsistent.

*Datum / Data* is an example of a Latin singular and plural. Other Latin and Greek words commonly used in scientific English are: *apex / apices, axis / axes, analysis / analyses, criterion / criteria, lemma / lemmata, optimum / optima, phenomenon / phenomena, vertex / vertices*.

#### 9.7 Nouns ending in -*s*

Some singular nouns finish in ‘s’. Such words behave in different ways:

*Economics, electronics, mathematics, physics, politics, statistics –* when these words describe a subject of study, they require a verb in its singular form (e.g. *is* not *are*).

- **Statistics is** a distinct mathematical science, rather than a branch.
- **Economics is** one of the most popular subjects amongst students in our university.
- **This means** of transport **is** the fastest.

If the words above are not used in the sense of a subject of study, they generally require the verb in the plural, but are also found with a verb in the singular. An exception is *electronics* which is found, indifferently, with a singular or plural verb.

- It is not clear where **these statistics come** from.

*Means* can be the plural of *mean* (i.e. average). However, *means* is singular when the meaning is *way*, for example, *a means of transport*.

- We calculated many samples. The standard errors of the **means** of the samples **were** …
- **Another means** ~~**/ mean**~~ of transport is the train.

*News* is uncountable, also medical words such as *diabetes, mumps,* and *pus* are uncountable.

- No news **is** ~~**/ are**~~ good news.
- Diabetes **is** ~~**/ are**~~ a chronic disease.

Nouns that end in *-is* form their plural with *-es* (e.g. *one analysis / thesis, two analyses / theses*).

- In their **theses** they conducted several **analyses** of …
- In my **thesis** I conducted an **analysis** of …

*Species* is both singular and plural.

- **These** two **species** ~~/ **specie**~~ are subdivided into serotypes.
- **This** species ~~/ **specie**~~ is very rare.

#### 9.8 Nouns indicating a group of people

Some nouns that have a plural form are often used in the singular (i.e. with no *s*), but with either a singular or a plural verb. Such nouns all relate to humans and include: *army* (*navy, air force*)*, audience, board, cabinet* (*council, government, senate* etc.), *class* (as in group of students), *committee, company* (*firm, corporation* etc.), *crew, department, faculty, family, jury, majority, media, minority, public, staff, team*. The choice of singular or plural depends on whether the people who make up the group are acting as individuals (generally plural verb preferred) or as a collective unit (generally singular verb).

- The class **is** made up of 15 students.
- The board of examiners **is / are** authorized to make decisions regarding …
- The board of examiners **is** ~~/ **are**~~ a statutory body established by the department.
- Manchester United **is** big football club <small>(i.e. the company / business)</small>
- Manchester United **is / are** playing Manchester City in the final. <small>(i.e. the club \[is\], the players \[are\])</small>

*Police* is followed by a verb in the plural.

- The police **are** ~~**/ is**~~ often perceived as being racist.

#### 9.9 *person* vs *people*

*People* requires a plural verb. *persons* is often used as a more formal version of *people*. *persons* is frequently found in medical and psychology research papers, or when talking about the capacity of a machine to hold a certain number of persons. In other cases *people* is often more appropriate particularly when it refers to people in general, rather than a subset.

- Under pressure, **many** ~~**/ much**~~ **people** admit that they believe in ghosts.
- Title: Prevention of heart disease in older **persons / people.**
- Title: A hypnotherapy treatment for **persons / people / those** prone to criminal activities
- Title: Job satisfaction – How do **people** ~~**/ persons**~~ feel about their jobs?

#### 9.10 Number-verb agreement

Usually the noun closest to the verb determines whether the verb is in a singular or plural form. Example: *The majority of* ***books have*** *now been digitized by Google.* In this example there are two nouns – *majority* and *books* – but *books* is closest to the verb (*have been digitized*).

- Around **40%** of the **funds have** ~~**/ has**~~ been deposited.
- The **majority** of **those** interviewed **were** ~~**/ was**~~ **Europeans**.
- Only a **quarter** of **these men are** ~~**/ is**~~ still alive.

*a number of* requires a verb in its plural form; *the number of* requires a verb in its singular form.

- **A number of papers have** ~~**/ has**~~ highlighted this major difference.
- **The number** of papers being published on this topic **has** ~~**/ have**~~ **increased.**

*a set of* or *a series of* requires a verb in its singular form.

- **A set** of three parameters **is** ~~**/ are**~~ obtained.
- **A series** of four experiments **was** ~~**/ were**~~ performed.

The verb before *more than one* should be in its singular form.

- This happens when there **is** ~~**/ are**~~ **more than one** possible answer.

#### 12.2 *any* vs *some*

Use *any* (and *anything, anyone, anywhere* etc) in negative phrases.

Use *some* (and *something, someone, somewhere* etc) in affirmative phrases.

- This did not give **any** interesting results.
- This gave **some** interesting results.

*Not … any* = zero, *not … some* = not all.

- We were **not** able to understand **any** of the figures – they were all too complicated and unclear.
- We were **not** able to fulfill **some** of the referees requests, specifically the first and last requests.

*Any* is used to indicate doubt, we are not sure whether the event will take place or not.

- The table shows significant results, if **any**, of each test.
- The table shows **some / a few** significant results, in fact …
- If you need **any** clarifications, then do not hesitate to contact me. <small>I don’t know if you require clarifications or not.</small>
- I need **some / a few** clarifications with regard to points 3 and 8.

If you use *any* in a sentence that contains no negation and is not expressing doubt, then it means ‘one thing or person at random from all the individuals in the world’.

- **Anyone** can tell you that one plus one equals two.

*some* and *someone* mean one particular thing or person, although exactly what or who is not important.

- **Someone** is at the door.

*Any* is used in questions where the answer is not known; *some* is used in questions where the expected answer is affirmative (e.g. in offers and some kinds of requests).

- **Any** book on the subject will tell you all you need to know.
- I read about it in **some** book, but I don’t remember which one.
- Excuse me, do you have **any** idea where the local mosque is?
- Would you like **some** wine?

#### 12.3 *any* vs *no*

*No one* is preferred to *not … anyone* in formal situations such as research papers.

- ~~To the best of our knowledge there is**n’t anyone** who has found …~~
- To the best of our knowledge **no one** has found similar results to these.

*Without* and *hardly* require *any* rather than *not.*

- ~~You can do this **without no** problems or at least with **hardly no** problems.~~
- You can do this **without any** problems or at least with **hardly any** problems.

#### 12.4 *a little, a few* vs *little, few*

*A little* (uncountable nouns) and *a few* (plural nouns) indicate a limited quantity of something. They can be replaced by *some.*

- We have **a little / some** time left, so does anyone else have any questions?
- We have **a few / some** more experiments to do, five or six I think, and then we have finished.

*Little* (uncountable nouns) and *few* (plural nouns) indicate an extremely low or surprisingly low number. They have a negative sense.

- **Little** is known about this very rare disease.
- **Few** researchers have investigated this complex phenomenon. <small>Maybe only two or three researchers.</small>

#### 12.5 *much, many, a lot of,* and *lots of*

*much* is used with uncountable nouns, and *many* with plural nouns.

- There is not **much information** ~~**/ many informations**~~ on this topic.
- We have not made **much progress** ~~**/ many progresses**.~~
- There have been **many advances** in this technology.

*lots of* and *a lot of* are not usually used in scientific texts, prefer *a great deal of.* However they are fine in presentations.

- We have **a great deal of / a considerable amount of** ~~**/ a lot of / lots of**~~ data on this issue.

*not much* or *not many* are used in negative phrases.

- There are **not many** accessible papers on this subject.

#### 12.6 *each* vs *every, every* versus *any*

*Each* is used when it is important to underline that you are identifying things as individual items, *every* when these things are seen as a totality.

- An acronym is a word in which **each letter** stands for another word.
- She is only two years of age and already knows **every letter** in the alphabet.
- **Each patient / Each individual patient** was given a slightly different dosage of the medicine.
- **Every patient / All the patients** in their hospital has/have medical insurance.
- It is **each applicant’s** responsibility to ensure that they provide references.
- What **every applicant** should know about the interview process.

Sometimes, there is no real difference between *each* and *every.*

- **Each /Every** we do the experiment something goes wrong.

Only *each* can be used before a preposition.

- **Each of** them has a different name.

Some expressions require *every* and not *each.*

- Patients will be examined **every** week / **every** three months / **every** third month.

*Any* = only one, but it is indifferent which one*, every =* all.

- **Any** element in a set can be used. <small>Just one element, it does not matter which one.</small>
- **Every element / All the elements** in this set is important.

#### 12.7 *no* vs *not*

*No* + *noun* and *not* + *a* / *the* + *noun* are similar in meaning.

- We have **no** reason to suppose that ….
- This is **not a good reason** for …

The form *not + noun* is incorrect.

- ~~We have **not** reason to suppose that ….~~

*No* + *noun* is often replaced with *not* + *verb* + *any* + *noun*.

- We **encountered no** problems with the calculations.
- We did **not encounter any** problems …

Adjectives that follow the verb *to b*e and which are not associated with a noun are generally preceded by *not.*

- There are **no unusual species** in this area.
- It is **not unusual** to find strange species in this area.
- It is **no surprise** that the cardiovascular system is the first organ system to reach a functional state in an embryo.

Use *not* before an adverb.

- **Not surprisingly**, the cardiovascular system is the first organ system to reach a functional state in an embryo.

*No longer* can be written more informally as *not* … *any more* / *longer.*

- This system is **no longer** used.
- This system is **not** used **any more**.
- This system is **not** used **any longer**.

*No* + *comparative adjective* means that the two things compared are equal.

*Not* + *comparative adjective* means that the first thing is not, for example bigger or stronger, than the second thing.

- Verifying X turns out to be **no easier** than verifying Y. <small>X and Y have the same level of difficulty.</small>
- X is **not easier** to solve than Y. <small>Y is probably easier to solve than Z.</small>

## Skill rules

Where the sources above offer alternatives, the output follows these choices. A target journal's own rule overrides them.

- **ASCII symbols.** Rule 11 in `core-principles.md` also covers symbols: write *degrees C* instead of the degree sign, *m2* instead of a superscript, *1/2* instead of a fraction character, and *u* for the SI prefix micro. Keep a symbol only where the content requires it (mathematics, units in a formula).
- **US forms.** Where the sources give British and US forms, use the US one: *percent*, *meter*, *liter* (Source B 6.1.9 and 10.4).
- **Dates.** Write the month as a word, never an all-figure date. The US order is *July 4, 1776* (Source B 7.1.1 in `connectors-time.md`; Source C 7.15).
- ***data* is plural.** Write *the data are* and *these data* (Source B 4.4.1; Source C 9.6).
