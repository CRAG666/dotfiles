### 16 Translation

This chapter gives a brief overview of how to use machine translation tools.

You will learn how to improve the chances of getting a good translation by pre-editing the source text (i.e. modifying the text in your own language) before submitting it for translation.

The chapter also indicates a few errors that machine translations often make.

It is worth remembering that human translators are also liable to make mistakes – a Google search will quickly show you some of the most famous examples, some of which had dire consequences.

More details on machine translation can be found in *AI-assisted Writing and Presenting in English* (2024). Exercises on using machine translation can be found in Chapter 13 of the 2024 edition of *Writing Exercises*.

Machine translation does NOT mean that you no longer need to learn English – see 16.3, 16.4 and the Introduction on page 223.

#### 16.1 Translation resources

There are many translation resources available.

##### machine translators

Some of the most commonly used machine translation services are:

- Google Translate: https://translate.google.com/
- DeepL: https://www.deepl.com/en/translator
- Microsoft Translator: https://www.microsoft.com/en- us/translator//
- Systran: https://www.systran.net/en/translate/
- Nice Translator: https://nicetranslator.com/
- Babelfish: https://www.babelfish.com/
- Baidu: https://fanyi.baidu.com/
- Youdai: https://fanyi.youdao.com/

Although not intended primarily as a translation, ChatGPT can also be used to translate sentences and short texts (16.7). To learn about the prompts you can use with a chatbot in order to submit a text for translation see: https://tefldiscussions. com/ai-prompts/

Machine translations are constantly improving. It can sometimes be difficult to understand whether a translation between two major languages was done automatically or by a human. Google Translate and DeepL tend to be more accurate than a non-native English writer when translating a formal text (e.g. academic, technical, scientific) from a major language (particularly French, German, Spanish and Italian) into English.

Machine translation tools are less accurate with emails, particularly informal ones, and are unlikely to do a good job on CVs / resumes.

In my opinion, Google Translate has the following advantages over some of the other services:

- it is free
- it is easier and quicker to use
- you can upload much longer documents than most other automatic translators
- you can edit the translated document without any restrictions (i.e. without having to pay for a premium service)

##### dictionaries and words in context

You can find the translation of an individual word by using online bilingual dictionaries e.g. WordReference, Cambridge, Oxford, Mirriam-Webster, Collins, Langenscheidt, Larousse, Zanichelli etc.

These dictionaries are easy to search, are updated regularly, and often contain links to discussion forums regarding the word you are searching for.

However, compared to sites such as Reverso (see 11.6) and Linguee, online dictionaries only show a limited number of examples of words in context, and these examples may not show the word you really need. Instead, Reverso will show you the same word used in many different contexts. You can then select the most appropriate word. You will also see a lot of new ways to translate a word that in the past you may have always translated in one particular way. Consequently, sites such as Reverso are a great way to learn English.

If you are doing a difficult technical or legal translation, and you can’t find what you think is an acceptable translation for a particular term, then you can try specialized translation services such as ProZ.com, where you can ask up to 15 questions a day and specify the field/discipline where examples of this word are likely to be found.

#### 16.2 Pre-editing: preparing your text for machine translation

You can massively increase the chances of getting an accurate machine translation, if you make the checks listed below. Such preparation is known as ‘pre-editing’.

##### check for inaccuracies in your original text

Clearly, if there are mistakes in the original text, these mistakes will be reflected in the translated text.

Ensure that:

1. all the words are spelled correctly
2. there is one space between words and that ~~twowords arenot~~ joined together
3. that ~~*words*~~ are not repeated
4. punctuation is used correctly. If used incorrectly it could affect the translation
5. words have an initial capital letter where required: e.g. *Student’s t-test* \[not ~~*student’s t-test*~~\]
6. there is grammatical agreement between subject and verb
7. the level of formality is consistent throughout the text so that the machine translator always chooses the most appropriate expression
8. accents are used correctly, and that a character with the accent on it is used rather than an apostrophe (e.g. *è* and not *e’*)

##### make your native language version sound/look more like english

Ten key ways of getting a more accurate and more natural-sounding translation from your language into English are to:

1. divide up long sentences into shorter sentences (13.1)
2. punctuate in an ‘English way’ (1.4–1.18)
3. remove redundant words, phrases, sentences even paragraphs (13.3, 13.4)
4. ensure that each phrase has only one possible interpretation, i.e. check for ambiguity (5.4, 5.5, 13.6–13.8, 15.11)
5. rearrange the word order by making it reflect English word order: subject + verb + object (all three parts as close as possible together without any intervening clause) – see Chapter 17
6. replace pronouns with the noun that they refer to (13.7)
7. replace vague words with specific words
8. use active sentences – this means that your phrase will contain a subject, and this subject must precede the verb (in English). Consequently, the automatic translator is likely to produce a more accurate translation. However, in some cases (e.g. in the Methods section of a paper), the passive is more appropriate (15.10)
9. do not use synonyms for key words (13.4)
10. do not use masculine pronouns to describe generic people (e.g. *the user … he …*), instead where possible use plural forms (*users … they*) – see 10.2

To learn how to produce more accurate translations using machine translators, the 2023 edition of *Writing Research Papers in English.*

Note: ALWAYS check the output of a machine translation. Sometimes the translator will even change the names of people and places or add a random phrase.

#### 16.3 Learn English while you type

If you are starting from scratch, i.e. you don’t already have a text ready to translate, then type directly into the left-hand window of your machine translator. This will enable you to see how your translation evolves: the translator will constantly be modifying the translation as you write your text.

In the screenshots below note how *between* becomes *among* as the translation progresses and more words are added. Note that *tra* is the Italian equivalent for both *between* and *among*.

> Screenshot (Italian → English): “Si è molto discusso tra” → “There has been much discussion between”
>
> Screenshot (Italian → English): “Si è molto discusso tra antropologi” → “There has been much discussion among anthropologists”

Initially, Google translates *fra* with *between*. However, when the plural words *anthropologists* and *sociologists* are introduced, Google correctly switches to *among* (2.4).

Below is an example from Chinese into English. It highlights how, at each of the four stages, when a new character is added this leads to a revision in the initial translation.

> Screenshot (Systran, Chinese → English): “非常” → “Very”; “非常感谢” → “Thank you very much”; “非常感谢您的课” → “Thank you so much for your lesson”; “非常感谢您的课程” → “Thank you very much for your course”

The idea is that as you type in your own language you can see the translation taking place in real time. Seeing how the translation corrects itself as you add new words should also help you improve your command of English.

NB: Not all machine translations go through the process shown in the screenshots.

This approach of translating ‘on the fly’, works very well for routine emails.

> Screenshot (nicetranslator, into English): “Vielen Dank für Ihre schnelle Antwort.” → “Thank you for your quick reply.”

You can then cut and paste the translation into your email.

A very useful feature of machine translation is that you can type in a mixture of your language and English. So if you know a particular expression in English, then you can write it in English, and the other parts of the email in your own language.

> Screenshot (DeepL, French → English (US)): “La notion de learning style repose sur l’idée que les individus ont des manières différentes d’apprendre, c’est-à-dire de percevoir, storing, traiter et restituer l’information, de construire a knowledge base (Chartier, 2003). Selon les études portant sur l’impact des styles” → “The notion of learning style is based on the idea that individuals have different ways of learning, i.e. of perceiving, storing, processing and retrieving information, of building a knowledge base (Chartier, 2003). According to studies on the impact of learning styles”

In the example above, the user has just typed in the words she knows in English. But she could also produce entire documents using a mix of French and English, i.e. some sections / paragraphs / sentences in English and some in French. The machine translator then merges everything into the target language.

#### 16.4 Improving your English through machine translators

Machine translators make fewer mistakes than most non-native English speakers when translating into English. For example, Google Translate does not make any of the mistakes written in red below.

- ~~When I will see her, I will tell her.~~
- When I see her, I will tell her.

> Screenshot (French → English): “Quand je la verrai, je lui dirai” → “When I see her, I'll tell her”

In the sentence above, *quand je la verrai, je lui dirai*, French uses the future tense in both parts of the phrase. Instead, English uses the present after *when*. As you can see, the machine translation is correct.

- ~~These informations are correct.~~
- This information is correct.

> Screenshot (German → English): “Diese Informationen sind interessant.” → “This information is interesting.”

In the sentence above, *Diese Informationen sind interessant*, German uses plural forms (literally: *these informations are*). Instead, English uses singular forms because the noun information is uncountable. Again, the machine translation is correct.

The mistakes in red above are the kind of mistakes you probably make if you do a literal translation from your language into English. Below are some other examples where you might make a mistake, whereas Google Translate would generate the correct version.

- ~~First, we **have weighed** the samples.~~
- First, we **weighed** the samples. <small>Correct tense.</small>
- ~~We used a **doctor experienced** to carry out the diagnosis.~~
- We used an **experienced doctor** to carry out the diagnosis. <small>Correct word order.</small>
- ~~The referee **suggested / recommended us to rewrite** the first section.~~
- The referee **suggested** / **recommended that we (should) rewrite** the first section. <small>Correct construction.</small>
- ~~**Even if** the instructions to the author stipulate a maximum of 250 words we used 300.~~
- **Even though / Although** the instructions to the author stipulate a maximum of 250 words we used 300. <small>Correct word.</small>
- ~~This technique was used in **an european** project.~~
- This technique was used in **a European** project. <small>Correct article and spelling.</small>

Some of the above mistakes will also be spotted and underlined by editors such as Word and Grammarly.

#### 16.5 General mistakes made in machine translations

Machine translators tend to work best with the major European languages, less so with Eastern European languages and Asian languages. However in the future, it is likely that other languages will also be translated to the same high levels as French, Italian, Spanish and German.

##### grammar mistakes

Below are a few mistakes made by machine translators at the time of writing this book.

- ~~This allows to reduce x.~~ <small>A different construction is required after *allow* – see 16.5.</small>
- ~~This allows **you** to reduce x.~~ <small>GT tends to write *you* rather than *us*. The use of *you* is not appropriate in a paper.</small>
- This allows **us** to reduce x.
- This allows **x to be reduced**.
- This **reduces** x.
- ~~Each test was replicated **3** times.~~ <small>Numerals from 1–9 tend to be written as words – see 7.3.</small>
- Each test was replicated **three** times.
- ~~The samples were **finally** incubated in …~~ <small>*finally* is in the wrong position – see 17.13</small>
- **Finally**, the samples were incubated in …

Another typical mistake is when a word in your language has more than one translation in English. For example, the Italian word for *sample* is *campione*, which also translates into *champion* in English. Usually the context will help the translator, but if you only type in a few words (rather than a complete paragraph or text), you may find that this type of error is made.

> Screenshot (Italian → English): “infine i campioni sono stati” → “finally the champions were”

Adding more words will generally resolve the problem.

> Screenshot (Italian → English): “infine i campioni sono stati incubati” → “finally the samples were incubated”

Note that the same machine translator may translate the same sentence in two different ways at different times, and one may be correct, both correct, or neither correct.

In the example below, Google Translate has failed to capitalize the first person pronoun *I*.

> Screenshot: “quand je la verrai, je lui dirai” → “when i see her i will tell her”

But on another occasion, Google Translate gets the capitalization correct, but has changed *her* to *him*.

> Screenshot: “Quand je le verrai, je lui dirai” → “When I see him, I will tell him”

##### mistakes when there are some words in english in a text in your native language

The next example shows what can go wrong when you have used English words in a text which is otherwise in your own language. In such cases, you need to be very careful that the machine translator does not then re-translate the English. The example below is from Microsoft Bing (the word *regards* is also a French word meaning *looks*).

> Screenshot (Microsoft Bing): “Dear Colleague, Afin d’accroître l’impact et la visibilité de la revue Mesure et Évaluation en Éducation (MEE), et dans une perspective d’ouverture au contexte bilingue du Canada, nous offrirons aux autrices ... .... We look forward to hearing from you. Kind regards.” → “Dear Colleague, In order to increase the impact and visibility of the journal Mesure et Évaluation en Éducation (MEE), and in a perspective of openness to the bilingual context of Canada, we will offer women authors ... .... We look forward to hearing from you. Kind looks.”

Sometimes, it is really important for you to leave certain words in English in the middle of a sentence that you have written in your native language. The reason might be that your language does not have its own word for this English word (typically a technical word). I suggest you highlight in yellow all the English words in a text that you have written in your own language. Then submit the entire document to translation, i.e. using the ‘upload document’ feature. The resulting translation will keep the format of your original, including the words highlighted in yellow. You can then quickly check against your original version that the translator has not ‘retranslated’ the yellow English words.

If you are doing a machine translation of your research paper, you should also highlight any names of authors that appear in the main text if such names are also commonly used words. For example, *Green* and *Baker*, are two common surnames in English, but *green* is also a color, and a *baker* is someone who makes bread. Consequently, these names may get inadvertently translated.

##### dangers of using machine translators to translate one individual word

I strongly suggest that you DO NOT use a machine translator to translate one individual word from your language into English.

Instead, insert the word in context into the machine translator, or use a site such as Reverso, see 11.4). This is because the same word can often have more than one meaning depending on the context.

e.g. I *live* in Manchester and I like *live* music.

In the first case, *I live* means *I am resident*, in the second case *live* means that *I like being in the audience at the concert hall*.

In the screenshot below (English to Portuguese), you can clearly see that *live* has two different translations in Portuguese.

> Screenshot (English → Portuguese): “I live in Manchester and I like live music.” → “Eu moro em Manchester e gosto de música ao vivo.”

So, it is always best to provide the context for the machine translator, i.e. show the word in context.

#### 16.6 Typical mistakes made in machine translations of emails

Each language has its own particular standard phrases. Machine translators do not always translate these standard phrases correctly and may use a literal translation rather than an appropriate equivalent.

##### salutations

Some phrases of this type cannot be translated literally (i.e. word for word) from one language to another. It is thus important that you learn the equivalent phrase.

Below in red are some machine translations of phrases from Turkish into English. Their equivalents in standard English are in blue.

- ~~Dear Mrs / Ms Gülgün~~
- Dear Bensu Gülgün / Dear Dr Gülgün
- ~~Good day, Mrs. Gülgün,~~
- Good morning / afternoon
- ~~I am sending the reference letter I wrote as an attachment.~~
- Please find attached my reference letter.

In some cases there is no exact equivalent and the machine translation may not reflect your intended meaning. The following two phrases in red are typically used to end an email in Turkish and Chinese, respectively. But they would not be used in English in the same way

- ~~Good work~~ <small>(Turkish – iyi çalışmalar)</small>
- ~~Good luck~~ <small>(Chinese – 祝好)</small>

The translation may also be related to what you have said in the rest of the email. So better translations would be:

- Keep up the good work. <small>If someone has been doing a good job and you want to encourage them to continue doing so.</small>
- Have a good day. <small>As a way of ending an email with no reference to work.</small>
- Kind regards <small>A typical neutral salutation (i.e. not too formal or too informal)</small>

##### punctuation

Punctuation conventions differ from language to language. Your machine translator is unlikely to correct such differences in pronunciation. For example, a typical way of ending an email in Chinese is to write: 祝好，谢谢!

This translates on Google Translate and https://fanyi.baidu.com/ (a Chinese automatic translator), as:

- ~~Best regards, thank you!~~
- ~~Good wishes, thank you!~~

Both translations contain the traditional Chinese exclamation mark (!), which would not be required in English. An English speaker would also be more likely to write: *Thank you again. Best regards*

Another convention in English is to begin the first word of the first line of the main text of an email with a capital letter.

- Dear Adrian,
- **Thanks** ~~**/ thanks**~~ for getting back to me.

If your language uses a different system, your machine translators may or may not get the English punctuation correct.

##### wrong level of formality

Be careful of phrases that are typically used in your language that may be inappropriate (e.g. too informal or too formal) when used in English. Also, do not use mix of levels of formality in the same phrase / email. The examples below are typical of English emails written by Indian students (*hi* is very informal)

- ~~Hi sir~~
- Dear Professor
- ~~Hi professor Smith~~
- Dear Professor Smith

Now let’s look at a complete email.

Below is an email translated from Japanese by Google Translate. It highlights some typical mistakes that machine translators make.

> I am writing to ask if I can stay at your research institute as a visiting research associate on research leave from September 2025 to July 2026. My name is **Sato Yamada**. **He** is currently an associate professor at the University of Science and Technology, Japan, at the Institute for **Wide Area Mathematics**. If my application is accepted, the Japanese Ministry of Education will fund my stay in the UK. **We** would appreciate it if you could send **us** an email with details of the proper application process at your convenience.
>
> **We** look forward to your reply.
>
> Best regards
>
> **Yamada** Sato

You can see that there are a few mistakes, particularly with pronouns: *he is = I am, we/us = I/me*. And the name of the person has been written in two different forms.

Below is the same email translated by a human with excellent Japanese-to-English skills.

> I am *writing with reference to the possibility of attending your institution as a visiting scholar on sabbatical* from September 2025 to July 2026. My name is Sato Yamada. I am an associate professor of *interdisciplinary mathematics* at the University of Science and Technology, Japan. If my application is successful, the Ministry of Education in Japan has agreed to provide funding for my stay in the UK. I would greatly appreciate it if you *could send me details* regarding the appropriate application procedure by email at your *earliest* convenience.
>
> *Looking forward to hearing from you.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> Sato Yamada

The main differences are in the level of formality: the human translation is more formal, which is likely more appropriate for this kind of request (though *Yours faithfully* may be even too formal).

##### spelling mistakes

Below is an email that has been Google-translated from Polish to English.

> Screenshot (Polish → English): “Szanowny Panie Profesorze, Dziękuję za niezwłoczną odpowiedź. Jest jakiś problem z serwerem UO, dlatego piszę z prywatnego adresu. Rzeczywiście, wygląda na to, że nie będzie możliwości dofinansowania - czasopismo nie jest punktowane w naukach medycznych. Z pozdrowieniami,” → “Dear Proffesor, Thank you for your prompt reply. There is a problem with the UO server, so I am writing from a private address. Indeed, it seems that there will be no possibility of funding - the journal is not scored in medical sciences. Greeting,”

Inexplicably there is a spelling mistake (*Proffesor*). Spelling mistakes are actually very rare. Moreover, *Greeting* should be *Best regards* (or at least *Greetings*, with the plural *s*). And *scored* should be *ranked*.

Moral of the story: If your email is a routine email to someone you already know, then a machine translator could save you time and will generally not make any spelling mistakes. However, when a document is really important, it is a good idea to have a native speaker check it for you or to pay a teacher or editing agency to check and revise it for you.

#### 16.7 Warning! The quality of machine translations also depends on the source language

The quality of a machine translation:

1. differs depending on the language – languages with fewer speakers are translated less, thus the machine translators have a smaller database and consequently produce lower quality translations
2. depends on the tool
3. depends on the quality of the original – if the source text is written badly, the translation will suffer as a consequence

I tested Points 1 and 2 above with ChatGPT.

ChatGPT doesn’t claim to be a machine translator, but in reality it does a reasonable job for short texts.

I wanted to give it a difficult test, so I used a language that is only spoken by seven million people – Turkmen. Given that the demand for translations involving Turkmen is very low, most other machine translation services, such as DeepL, do not offer translations from or into Turkmen.

Both tools made some serious errors. For instance, Chat translated one phrase as *natural gas resources* and Google as *archeological treasures*, whereas the correct version should have been *mineral resources*. This highlights that with ‘minor’ languages, machine translations can be inaccurate and can differ considerably from each other.

On the other hand, with ‘major’ languages, such as Spanish and French, different translation services tend to output very similar translations, which are generally very high quality.

Final word. If you use ChatGPT to translate from a minor language into your own, and this minor language is not translatable by any other machine translator, then be careful with the result.

For example, I inserted a sentence in Georgian, a language currently spoken by around four million people. ChatGPT translated the sentence as:

- Georgians disapprove of human rights violations and believe that people who commit them should be held accountable and punished for their actions.

But the real meaning (as translated – slightly imperfectly – by a native speaker of Georgian) is:

- Georgians pay attention to a person’s appearance. They consider doing this as they care about others giving them aesthetic pleasure, and thus increase their self-esteem.

Several times I asked Chat to re-do the translation, but its output was just a variation of its original output. When I later asked Chat whether it was able to translate from Georgian into English, it responded:

- Yes, I can translate Georgian into English. Please provide the text that you would like me to translate.

Basically, Chat did NOT translate the text, but found a highly political sentence and used that. There is no conspiracy behind this, but it is a little worrying.
