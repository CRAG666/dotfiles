### 17 Word order

In this section many of the sentences in red are not grammatically wrong, but they are not the optimal way of expressing the concept. However, in cases where the sentence is red is totally incorrect, these are marked with an asterisk (~~\*~~).

#### 17.1 Subject then verb

Put the subject before the verb.

- ~~They have arrived **the referees’ reports**. \*~~
- **The referees’ reports** have arrived.
- ~~It is important the **method**. \*~~
- The **method** is important.
- ~~Among the factors that influence the choice of parameters are **time and cost**.~~
- **Time and cost** are among the factors that influence the choice of parameters.

#### 17.2 Do not delay the subject

Put the subject first before mentioning when, how, where and why it functions. If you begin with a subordinate clause, this will force the reader to wait in order to find out what you are really referring to.

- ~~After five minutes in an aqueous solution and 20 minutes in the cold room the **samples** were dried.~~
- The **samples** were dried after they had spent five minutes in an aqueous solution, and 20 minutes in the cold room.
- ~~Despite **its** favorable geological situation in terms of harnessing all kinds of geothermal resources, until a few years ago only geothermal-electric generation received much attention in **Iceland**.~~
- Despite **Iceland’s** favorable geological situation in terms of harnessing all kinds of geothermal resources, until a few years ago only geothermal-electric generation received much attention.
- ~~Although in the above references one can find **algorithms** for this kind of processing, the execution of …~~
- Although **algorithms** for this kind of processing are reported in the above references, the execution of …

If you are using a lot of link words, such as *generally speaking, consequently, in addition*, don’t always put them at the beginning. If possible, find a short word (*thus, so, also*) and insert it before the verb.

- ~~**For this reason**, it is not a good idea to use the **old system**.~~
- **The old system** should **thus** not be used.

Avoid using an impersonal *it* at the beginning of the sentence. Instead use modal verbs (*might, need, should* etc.) or an adverb.

- ~~**It is recommended** that users should be distributed evenly.~~
- Users **should** be distributed evenly.
- ~~**It is possible** do this with the new system.~~
- This **can** be done with the new system.

Avoid beginning a phrase with a time period containing the verb *to be.*

- ~~**They are three years** that we study this problem. \*~~
- ~~**It is since three years** that we study this problem. \*~~
- We have been studying this problem **for three years.**
- **For three years** we have been studying this problem and we still have no results

#### 17.3 Avoid long subjects that delay the main verb

Make sure the verb is near the beginning of the sentence and next to the subject. If the subject is very long, the reader will be left waiting to know what the verb is. To avoid this problem:

Use an active verb, rather than the passive form.

- ~~People with a high rate of intelligence, a proven talent for problem-solving, a passion for computers, along with good communication skills **are generally employed** by ABC.~~
- **ABC generally employs** people with a high rate of intelligence, a proven talent for problem-solving, a passion for computers, along with good communication skills.

Shift the verb to the beginning of the sentence. This may involve changing the verb and / or changing the word order.

- ~~This data shows that significant correlations between the cost and the time, the time and the energy required, and the cost and the age of the system **exist.**~~
- This data shows that **there are** significant correlations between …

Divide up a long sentence into two shorter sentences.

- ~~People with a high rate of intelligence, a proven talent for problem-solving, a passion for computers, along with good communication skills **are generally employed** by ABC.~~
- People with a high rate of intelligence **are generally employed** by ABC. They must also have other skills including: a proven talent for problem-solving …

#### 17.4 Put direct object before indirect object

The direct object is the thing given or received. The indirect object (underlined in the examples below) is the thing that the direct object is given to or received by.

Look at the position of the *direct object* and indirect object in this sentence:

The authors sent *their manuscript* to the journal.

The word order is: (1) subject (*the authors*), (2) verb (*sent*), (3) direct object (*their manuscript*), (4) preposition (*to*), (5) indirect object (*the journal*)*.*

The kind of construction outlined above is often found with verbs followed by *to* and *with.*

Examples: *associate X with Y, apply X to Y, attribute X to Y, consign X to Y, give X to Y (or give Y X), introduce X to Y, send X to Y* (or *send Y X*)

- ~~We can separate, with **this tool**, P and Q.~~
- We can separate P and Q with **this tool**.
- With **this tool** we can separate P and Q.
- ~~We can associate with **these values** a high cost. \*~~
- We can associate a high cost with **these values**.

If the direct object is very long and consists of a series of items, you can put the indirect object after the first item and then use *along with.*

As an alternative, use a colon to introduce a list.

- ~~We can associate with **these values** a high cost, higher overheads, a significant increase in man hours and several other problems. \*~~
- We can associate a high cost with **these values**, along with higher overheads, a significant increase in man hours and several other problems.
- We can associate several factors with **these values**: a high cost, higher overheads, …

Note: In mathematical sciences you may occasionally find the indirect object before the direct object.

- This is a rule that associates with **each element in S** a unique element in T.

#### 17.5 Avoid parenthetical phrases

Subjects often get separated from their verbs by parenthetical information contained between two commas or in parentheses. In such cases, the use of commas and parentheses breaks the flow of the sentence and makes it harder to understand immediately*.* In such cases, rearrange the sentence so that the subject and verb are next to each other. The order you use will depend on the emphasis you want to give.

- ~~**This feature**, owing to its high cost, **will** only be of limited use.~~
- ~~**This feature** (owing to its high cost) **will** only be of limited use.~~
- **This feature will** only be of limited use, owing to its high cost.
- Owing to its high cost, **this feature will** only be of limited use.
- ~~The **vegetables**, cooked in the oven, **were served** with the main course.~~
- ~~The **vegetables,** which had been cooked in the oven, **were served** with the main course.~~
- The **vegetables were cooked** in the oven **and then** served with the main course.

When the parenthetical information is long, split the sentence up.

- ~~The analysis of the results, **which we believe are of a significant value given their innovative nature**, should help in the understanding of the diffusion of this virus in the world today.~~
- **We believe** the results are significant given their innovative nature. **When** they are analyzed they should help in our understanding of the diffusion of this virus in the world today.

#### 17.6 Deciding which noun to put first in strings of nouns

Unfortunately there are no clear rules regarding which noun should go first. Also, the convention varies from discipline to discipline.

In many cases the first noun acts as an adjective that describes the second noun. In such cases the generic noun will normally go in second position.

- Press the **Control key**.
- Use **Track Changes** to make your revisions.

When talking about families it depends on whether you are talking about families of people, or families of insects, flowers etc. With human families use *surname + family*. In entomology, botany etc. use *family + species.*

- More has been written about the **Kennedy family** than perhaps any other family in the history of the United States.
- These mites are included in the **family** ***Tetranychidae***, Order Acarina, Class Arachnida.

#### 17.7 *noun + noun* and *noun + of + noun* constructions

In some cases, you can use either a *noun + of + noun* construction (e.g. *the University of Manchester*), or a *noun + noun* construction (e.g. *Manchester University*). Unfortunately there is no rule to help you decide whether they are both applicable or whether they both have the same meaning. For rules on when *’s* should be used, see genitive (Chapter 4).

- ~~**Payment’s** methods \*~~
- **Methods of** payment / **Payment** methods
- ~~A **nature law** / A **nature’s law \***~~
- A **law of nature**
- ~~A program of software and a **device of hardware \***~~
- A **software program** and a **hardware device**
- ~~San Francisco streets / San Francisco’s streets \*~~
- The streets **of** San Francisco.
- For **reasons of space** ~~**/ space reasons \***,~~ we will not consider …

The *noun + of + noun* construction is generally used with words such as *piece, series, bunch, group* and *herd* (*flock* etc.).

- ~~The **plug series** was used together with two **switch groups** and an innovative **electrical equipment piece**. \*~~
- The **series of plugs** was used together with two **groups of switches** and an innovative **piece of electrical equipment**.

Long strings of nouns and adjectives are generally only used if they are names of pieces of equipment or methods.

- A recently developed reverse Monte Carlo quantification method.
- A Hitachi S3500N environmental scanning electron microscope.

#### 17.8 Strings of nouns: use prepositions where possible

A string of nouns is a sequence of words made up of several nouns (and possibly adjectives) with no verbs or prepositions.

Do not put nouns in strings when the reader is unlikely to be able to understand how the various words relate to each other. Use prepositions to make the meaning clearer. This is particularly important in titles of papers – if the reader cannot understand your title then they will probably not read the paper.

A noun string can often be broken up by using a preposition: *of* = which belongs to, *for* = for the purpose of, *by* = how something is done and where necessary converting the nouns into verbs. This helps to clarify the relationships between the various nouns.

- ~~Least Toxic Pest Control Methods~~
- ~~Pest Control Least Toxic Methods \*~~
- Least Toxic Methods **for** Pest Control
- ~~Silicon wafer mechanical strength **measurement** for surface damage quantification.~~
- Quantifying surface damage **by measuring** the mechanical strength of silicon wafers.
- ~~Potato boiling instructions \*~~
- Instructions **for boiling** potatoes

#### 17.9 Adverbs of frequency + *also, only, just, already*

Adverbs of frequency (e.g. *always, sometimes, occasionally*) and words like *also, just, already,* and *only,* are usually placed:

- Immediately before the main verb.
- Immediately before the second auxiliary when there are two auxiliaries.
- After the present and past tenses of *to be.*

- ~~You need **only / also / just** to sign the document.~~
- You **only / also / just** need to sign the document.
- We don’t **usually** go ~~**/ don’t go usually**~~ abroad on holiday.
- We would **never** have seen him otherwise.
- ~~This may not have been **always** the case.~~
- This may not **always** have been the case.
- They are **always late /** ~~**late always**~~ in sending their manuscripts to the editor.

Note the difference.

- **Only** Emma has been to Japan. <small>No one else has been to Japan.</small>
- Emma has **only** been to Japan. <small>But she has not been to China or Korea.</small>

For special emphasis, some adverbs (*sometimes, occasionally, often, normally, usually*) can be located at the beginning of a sentence.

- **Normally** X is used to do Y, but **occasionally** X can be used to do Z.

#### 17.10 Adverbs of probability

Adverbs of probability (e.g. *probably, certainly, definitely*) go immediately before the main verb and before the negation (*not* and contractions e.g. *don’t, won’t, hasn’t*).

- She will **certainly** ~~**/** She **certainly** will~~ come.
- ~~She will **not** come **probably.**~~
- She **will probably not** come.
- She **probably won’t** come.

#### 17.11 Adverbs of manner

An adverb of manner describes how something is done (e.g. *quickly*), or to what extent (e.g. *completely*). Some adverbs of manner can go before the verb. But, since all adverbs of manner can always also go after the verb or noun, it is best to put them there. You will then avoid mistakes.

Subject + verb + adverb of manner + full stop (.).

- This program could **help substantially** ~~**/ substantially help**.~~
- This program will help **system administrators considerably** ~~**/ considerably** system administrators.~~

Subject + verb + noun + adverb \[+ rest of phrase\].

- ~~This program will help **considerably** system administrators to do x, y and z.~~
- This program will help system administrators **considerably** to do x, y and z.

#### 17.12 Adverbs of time

Adverbs of time usually go at the end of the phrase, particularly if they consist of more than one word.

- ~~**Once or twice a week / as soon as possible** we will go there.~~
- We will go there **once or twice a week / as soon as possible**.
- ~~We will **immediately** go there. / We will go **immediately** there.~~
- We will go there **immediately**.
- ~~**Tomorrow morning** we will go there, **not tomorrow evening**.~~
- We will go there **tomorrow morning, not tomorrow evening**.

In some cases (e.g. *today, tomorrow, tomorrow evening*) they can go at the beginning for emphasis.

- ~~We **today** are going to talk about the position of adverbs.~~
- **Today**, we are going to talk about the position of adverbs.

#### 17.13 Adverbs with more than one meaning

There are a few adverbs that change meaning depend on their position (i.e. before or after the verb):

*Clearly*: before = *obviously*, after = *without difficulty.*

- **Clearly**, the authors have not followed the instructions carefully.
- The instructions were not written **clearly**, in fact they were almost impossible to understand.

*Fairly*: before = *quite* (in a sufficient manner), after = *in the right proportion.*

- The article is written **fairly** well, but needs improving in several areas.
- Profits were not distributed **fairly** amongst the shareholders, which led to several complaints.

*Finally*: when *finally* follows *firstly, secondly* etc, it is positioned at the beginning of the sentence (17.14). However, it can also mean ‘after many difficulties’, in which case it is placed before the verb (this use is not found in academic manuscripts).

- **Finally**, the samples were incubated in …
- After 36 hours of traveling, we **finally** reached our destination,

*Normally*: before = *usually*, after = the opposite of *abnormally* (this usage is not very common, *in the normal way* is more common).

- Patients **normally** undergo rehabilitation after such accidents.
- After six months of rehabilitation 65% of the patients were able to walk **normally** (i.e. without assistance).

#### 17.14 *first(ly), second(ly), then* etc.

When you are listing events, put the adverb (*firstly, secondly* etc.) at the beginning of the phrase.

You can say *firstly* or *first, secondly, thirdly, fourthly* etc. are preferred to *second*, third, fourth etc. in a manuscript. *first* is usually followed by *then* rather than *secondly.*

*Then* tends not to be placed at the beginning of the sentence.

- ~~We will **firstly** do X. Then we will do Y. We will **finally** do Z.~~
- **First / Firstly**, we will do X. We will **then** do Y. **Finally**, we will do Z.

#### 17.15 Adjectives

Adjectives generally go before the noun they describe.

If you put the adjective after the noun, then precede the adjective with *that / which / who + verb.*

- ~~He is a **student intelligent** enough to pass the exam.~~
- He is an **intelligent** student.
- He is a **student who is intelligent** enough to pass the exam.
- ~~This solution has drawbacks **more serious** than the other solution.~~
- This solution has **more serious** drawbacks than the other solution.

*available* is normally located after the noun.

- The **software available** ~~**/ available software**~~ does not solve this problem.

Adjectives are not usually found between two nouns.

- ~~The software **main** features.~~
- The **main** features of the software.

Do not put an adjective before a noun that it does not describe.

- ~~The **main document** contribution.~~
- The **main contribution** of the document.

Adjectives are located after past participles.

- ~~They were **red and white colored**.~~
- They were **colored red and white**.

#### 17.16 Use *which* or *that* when adjectives follow their nouns

In English, adjectives generally precede the nouns (e.g. *a big house* not ~~*a house big*~~). If you want to put an adjective after the noun, then *which / that* are used (e.g. a house that is big).

In the red examples all the adjectives appear after their related nouns, whereas they should be before.
- ~~Spain, France and Italy present patterns **more evident** than Germany and UK.~~
- Spain, France and Italy present patterns **that are more evident** than Germany and UK. /
- Spain, France and Italy present **more evident** patterns than Germany and UK.
- ~~These are based on polyester resins **suitably activated**.~~
- These are based on polyester resins **that have been activated**.
- ~~The collaboration **already existing** between the two institutes will be exploited to …~~
- The collaboration **that already exists** between the two institutes will be exploited to …
- The **current** collaboration between the two institutes will be exploited to …

#### 17.17 Be clear which noun an adjective refers to

If an adjective is followed by two nouns, it may not be clear to the reader if the adjective only refers to the first noun, or both to the first and second nouns. If there could be ambiguity, then you need to rearrange the phrase.

- ~~The course is intended for **new students** and **teachers**.~~ <small>Is it just the students who are new, or also the teachers?</small>
- The course is intended for **new students** and **new teachers**.
- The course is intended for **new students** and **existing teachers**.

#### 17.18 Multiple adjectives

A very general guideline for a string of adjectives is: size + age + color + origin + material + use.

When deciding the order, first choose the main adjective (or noun acting as an adjective) which is typically found with the associated noun e.g. *software solutions*. Then precede with a maximum of three more adjectives e.g. *an extremely effective (and) innovative software solution*. In this example both *effective* and *innovative* have a similar function and are interchangeable in terms of the order they appear in. On the other hand, *extremely* relates to both *effective* and *innovative* and must therefore go before these two adjectives.

- ~~A red old English cotton large swimming costume.~~
- His swimming costume, which was large, old and red, was made in England and from cotton. It was found in …
- His large old red English cotton swimming costume was found in …
- ~~The **mean air daily temperature** was measured.~~
- The **mean daily air temperature** was measured.
- ~~The **stock low size** of **Asian edible species** has led to the need for new resources overseas.~~
- The **low stock size** of **edible Asian species** has led to the need for new resources overseas.

To aid clarity, consider changing an adjective into a noun, and modifying the word order.

- ~~All the **examined jellyfish ready-to- eat products** had been contaminated.~~
- All the **ready-to-eat jellyfish products that were examined** had been contaminated.

#### 17.19 *the more … the more*

The verb is placed after the subject, not before.

- ~~In realistic conditions, the more **is robust the software**, the fewer problems there are.~~
- In realistic conditions, the more **robust the software is**, the fewer problems there are.
- In realistic conditions, the more **robust the software**, the fewer problems.

The definite article (*the*) is required before each comparative.

- ~~**More** you use the software, **easier** it becomes.~~
- **The more** you use the software, **the easier** it becomes.

On some occasions, no verb is required in the second part of the sentence.

- ~~**The sooner** the job is done, **better is.**~~
- **The sooner** the job is done, **the better.**

#### 17.20 Past participles

In most cases past participles can always go after the noun, but in many cases they CANNOT go before. So, put them after and you will probably be right!

In some cases both positions are possible, though when the past participle is located after the noun it is often followed by further details.

- It shows details of all the **results found** ~~/ **found results**~~**.**
- ~~We detail the main **involved social actors** along with all the **consumed materials**.~~
- We detail the main **social actors involved** along with all the **materials consumed**.
- The **data reported / reported data** show that …
- It shows details of all the **specified actions**.
- It shows details of all the **actions specified** in the manual.

Be careful with *used*. Before the noun it means ‘second hand’, after the noun it means ‘which is used’.

- I bought a **used car**. <small>i.e. a second-hand car</small>
- This was the **application used** ~~**/ used application**~~ by the testers.

#### 17.21 Shift the negation word (*no, not, nothing* etc.) to near the beginning of the phrase

Negations and words with a potentially negative meaning, e.g. *rarely, unfortunately* should be located as near as possible to the beginning of the sentence. By doing so, you signal to the reader that you are about to say something negative rather than something affirmative. It can be misleading to put the negation at the end.

The sentences in *red italics* are correct but not optimal.

- ~~This seemed **not** to be the case.~~
- This **did not** seem to be the case.
- ~~The number of times this happens when the user is online is generally **very few**.~~
- ~~The frequency of this event when the user is online is **rare**.~~
- This **rarely** happens when the user is online.
- ~~*Documentation on this particular matter* ***is almost completely lacking****.*~~
- There is **almost no** documentation on this particular matter.
- ~~*Our results revealed that a relationship between X and Y does* ***not*** *exist.*~~
- Our results revealed that there is **no** relationship between X and Y.

Put *not* and *no* near the main verb, and also adverbs that contain negative information, for example: *only, rarely, seldom, never.*

- ~~We found to contradict these results **nothing**.~~
- We did **not** find **anything** to contradict these results.
- We found **nothing** to contradict these results.
- ~~Finally, a noticeable post-copulatory behavior was **not** observed in this species.~~
- Finally, **no** noticeable post- copulatory behavior was observed in this species.
- ~~The referees found the results **not** interesting.~~
- The referees **did not find** the results interesting.
- ~~*We realized this* ***only*** *at the end of the tests.*~~
- We **only** realized this at the end of the tests.

Note the position of *or not* when associated with *whether* + verb.

- ~~This study investigates the influences affecting a physician’s decision **whether** to prescribe **or not** medicines.~~
- This study investigates the influences affecting a physician’s decision **whether or not** to prescribe medicines.
- This study investigates the influences affecting a physician’s decision **whether** to prescribe medicines **or not**.

Double negations are rare in English.

- The device was **not** designed to be connected to a network, **either** ~~**/ neither**~~ wired **or** ~~**/ nor**~~ wireless.
- The authors did **not** write **anything** ~~**/ nothing**~~ regarding …
