### 2 Adverbs and prepositions

#### 2.1 *above (below), over (under*)

*Above* and *below* are typically used in a paper to refer to the location of sentences, paragraphs, figures and tables;

- As mentioned **above** there are three main methods, which are summarized in the table **below**:

*Above* and *below* are also used when referring to levels, lists, averages and hierarchies.

- Pisa is 50 m **above** sea level which is **below** the national average for Italian cities.

*Over* has a similar meaning to *cover*, i.e. there is often physical contact between two elements.

- A sheet was placed **over** the patient’s body.

*Over* and *under* also have a similar meaning to *more than* and *less than*, respectively.

- Only children **over** the age of 13 were considered in the sample. Those **under** 12 years of age will be the subject of a future investigation.

*Under* also means ‘in conformance with’.

- **Under** the new regulations, all such documents have to be filed **under** ‘funds’.

Note the difference between *above all* (i.e. the most important thing) and *overall* (i.e. globally).

- Many points need to be considered, **above all** age and sex.
- **Overall**, our results can be considered as an important step towards finding a cure for this endemic disease.

#### 2.2 *across, through*

*across* indicates the joining of two points on a plane.

*through* indicates a transversal motion with some kind of penetration.

- They swam **across** the river.
- The train went **through** the tunnel.
- They walked **across** the road.
- The sample was filtered **through** a very fine mesh.

*across* also means ‘not restricted to one particular area’.

- Our method can be applied **across** disciplines.

*through* can also be used to mean *by means of.*

- We discovered this **through** lengthy research.

#### 2.3 *already, still, yet*

*already* means at some time in the past. Do not confuse *already* with *just*. *just* means something that happened very recently (possibly a few seconds ago), e.g. *we have just arrived at the airport.*

- This procedure has **already** ~~**/ just**~~ been explained elsewhere \[Ying, 2024\].
- As **already** mentioned (see Sect 2.3), this method consists of ...

*yet* is frequently found in the affirmative and negative forms and refers to a period that started in the past and progresses up to (and possibly beyond) the present moment.

- Has our paper been reviewed **yet**?
- Your paper has not been reviewed **yet**, and is scheduled for review on 2 June.
- As **yet**, no progress has been made in this field ...

*still* has the same meaning as *yet*, but is stronger. It indicates that a situation has not changed and may suggest surprise or concern.

- We **still** haven’t heard from the referees. I am worried that they never received the paper, though I suppose they are **still** in time to contact us.
- Despite sustained pressure by the democratic movement, his dictatorship **still** survives intact.
- <small>= No progress has been made **yet**.</small>

*already, yet* and *still* can also be used with the **past perfect** to put two past events in relation.

- We **had already seen** her presentation before so we **did not want** to go again,
- When we got the conference room, the presenter **had not arrived yet.**
- Twenty minutes later, the presenter **had still not arrived.**

#### 2.4 *among, between, from, of* (differentiation and selection)

*between* when talking about a well-defined or well-separated number of items. It is found with verbs such as *decide, differentiate, discriminate, distinguish*, *mediate* and *synchronize*, and nouns such as *agreement, comparison, difference, distinction, interaction* and *relationship*. This is because such verbs and nouns indicate that a known number of items are involved.

*among* when the group of items is not easily separable or the number is not known or is not important.

- The money was divided up **between** the three winners. <small>= a specific number of people</small>
- The money was divided up **among** the participants. = <small>an unknown or unspecified number of people</small>
- We found no interaction in the classroom **between** teachers and students.
- Students were encouraged to discuss their assignments **among** themselves.
- Their paper discusses caste and social stratification **among** Muslims in India.
- We analyze the relationships **between** Hindus and Muslims in India.
- Many species have died out, **among** them X, Y and Z.
- The two parties will have to sort out the differences **between** them.

*from* with verbs such as *choose, pick, select,* and adjectives such as *different.*

- Candidates will be chosen **from** diverse disciplines and then selected **from** a shortlist of 10.

*of* is found at the beginning of a sentence when introducing a particular element that is part of a group.

- **Of** the three candidates we interviewed, the last was certainly the best.
- We selected our samples **from** a collection of 4543 items.

*of* is used when choosing from a number.

- A comparison **of** the three figures reveals that two thirds **of** tropical soils are oxisols and ultisols.
- Nine out **of** ten.
- Half **of** the samples.

#### 2.5 *at, in, to* (location, state, change)

*at* before buildings and work places.

*in* before towns, countries etc. In both cases no movement is involved.

- They arrived **at** the airport, while we were still **at** work and Pete was **at** the restaurant.
- They arrived **in** New York, while we were still **in** China.

*to* after a verb that indicates a destination.

- They have gone **to** Beirut for a conference.
- This was then moved **to** the top / bottom of the list.

*at* when describing the location of items in diagrams and figures.

*in* before figures, tables etc. when used in association with verbs such as *see, show, highlight.*

- This can be seen **at** the top / bottom / side / edge of the figure.
- As can be seen **in** Figure the trend is ... Also, as highlighted **in** Table 3 ...

*in* is used before certain states e.g. *in equilibrium, in parallel, in series.*

*to* is also used after certain adjectives that indicate position: *adjacent, close, contingent, contiguous, external, internal, next, orthogonal, parallel, perpendicular, tangent, transverse.*

- The devices are placed **in** parallel and operate **in** a steady-state manner.
- The lines are parallel **to** each other.

*to* indicates movement, change, conformance, limits and consequence: *adhere*, *adjust, attach, attract, bind, bring, come, confine, conform, connect, consign, convey, deliver, direct, email, fax, go, lead, link, move, react reply, respond, restrict, send, stick, supply, switch, take, tend, tie, transmit, write, yield*. This rule also applies to the related nouns: *delivery, modification, response, tendency* etc.

- This material **adheres** well **to** plastic, is **attracted to** metal, can be **bound to** polymers, **responds** well **to** high temperatures, and has a **tendency to react** well **to** stress.

#### 2.6 *at, in* and *on* (time)

*at* with a time of day, and with specific periods (*the weekend, Easter, Christmas* – where Easter and Christmas include the days before and after).

- The meeting is scheduled to start **at** 15.30.
- We usually take our holidays **at** Easter or **at** Christmas, and of course **at** the weekend.

*on* with a day. Note some native speakers say *on the weekend* others *at the weekend.*

- I will contact you **on** Monday morning.
- We do not work **on** Christmas Day, **on** Diwali, and **on** July 4 (Independence Day).

*in* with a period of time (week, month, year, decade, century etc.), including historical periods (*in the Middle Ages, in the Renaissance* etc.), and with *meantime* / *meanwhile.*

- The conference will be held **in** June.
- The last conference on this topic was held **in** 2025, and the previous one **in** the 2010s. The first was held **in** the 18th century.

#### 2.7 *at, to* (measurement, quality)

*at* with nouns that indicate quantity and measurement, e.g. *degree, interval, level, node, point, pressure, ration, speed, stage, temperature, velocity.*

*to* with the other types of calculations and measurements, e.g. with the following verbs: *approximate, calculate, correct, heat, measure, raise.*

- Water boils **at** a temperature of 100 C.
- Heat the water **to** a temperature of 50 C.
- The vehicle moves **at** a velocity of 300 cm / h.
- The potassium content was approximated **to** 90 mEq / kg.

*to* is used with certain adjectives that indicate a quality, conformance or similarity: *inferior, superior; equal, identical, proportional, similar; immune, impermeable, open, resistant, sensitive*; *according, alternative, analogous, attention, common, comparable, conformance, compliance, correspondence, entitlement, identical, inferior, likened, open, opposed, proportional, relative, relevant, responsive, similar, suited, superior, transparent.*

- Gender is common **to** all Latinate languages, but has no adherence **to** logical rules.

#### 2.8 *before, after, beforehand, afterwards, first* (time sequences)

*Before* and *after* must precede either a noun / pronoun, a gerund, or an entire subordinate phrase.

- **Before checking** ~~**/ to check**~~ the levels, the presence of any metals should be detected.
- Preparations should be made **before** the mixture becomes solid.

*Before* and *after* cannot be used as conjunctions or adverbs. Instead, use *beforehand* and *afterwards.*

- Where are you going **after** the congress?
- We’re going for a drink and **afterwards** ~~**/ after**~~ back to the hotel.
- Preparations should be made **beforehand** ~~**/ before**~~.
- The solution consists in detecting the presence of metals **beforehand** ~~**/ before**~~ and then checking the levels.

*First* means ‘before anything else’, it is often followed by *second(ly)* or *then*. It is thus used to list a sequence of actions.

- **First** we are going for a drink, **then** afterwards back to the hotel.
- **First(ly)** we detect the metals, **secondly** we check the metals, and finally we ...
- **First** the preparations should be made, **then** the mixture should be allowed to become solid.

#### 2.9 *beside, next to, near (to), close to* (location)

*beside* and *next to* have the same meaning and indicate elements that are touching or almost touching.

- I sat **beside / next to** her at the conference.

*Near (to)* and *close to* have the same meaning and indicate elements that are at some distance from each other.

- Our hotels were quite **near to / close to** each other, but on opposite sides of the river.

*Nearby* / *close by* replace *near* and *close* at the end of a phrase.

- There was a train station **nearby / close by.**

#### 2.10 *by* and *from* (cause, means and origin)

*by* when the agent of an action is mentioned.

*from* when the origin is mentioned.

- Our paper has now been revised **by** a native English speaker.
- We received a letter of acceptance **from** the editor.
- The original computers were made **by** IBM but were then replaced **by** the director.

Verbs typically followed by *from* are: *arise, benefit, borrow, deduce, defend, deviate, differ, ensue, exclude, originate, profit, protect, release, remove, select, separate, shield, subtract, suffer.* Some of the nouns that derive from these verbs are also found with *from: deviation, exclusion, protection.*

- This mixture is made **from** a variety of substances **from** all over the world.
- Taxes were raised **by** the government.
- The economic crisis arose **from** banking malpractices and indiscriminate consumer-borrowing **from** banks.
- Considerable damage was caused **by** the earthquake.
- This paper suffers **from** a lack of detailed discussion and would also benefit **from** a complete revision of the English.
- The number was then divided or multiplied **by** 32, depending on the case.
- The corresponding amount was obtained by subtracting the first value **from** the second.

*by* when the method or means is given.

- They learned English **by** watching videos on YouTube.
- They learned English **from** ~~**/ by**~~ their native-speaking colleagues.

*from* is often found with *to*, to indicate movement from one place to another.

- We went **by** train instead of going **by** car or **by** plane.
- While on the train **from** Malmo **to** Stockholm, they kept switching **from** one language **to** another.

#### 2.11 *by, in, of* (variations); *by* and *within* (time)

When talking about increases, decreases, modifications, changes. variations etc., use *by* after a verb, *in* with a noun, *of* with a number.

- The stock market has risen **by** 213 points.
- There has been an increase **in** inflation.
- There has been an increase in inflation **of** 5%.
- Attendance has fallen **by** 16%.
- A fall **in** unemployment is predicted.
- This was affected by variations **of** 16% and more.

*by* with an end date, *within* for a period.

- We must receive your manuscript **by** January 21 or at the latest **by** the end of the month.
- Manuscripts will be reviewed **within** six weeks of receipt.

#### 2.12 *by now, for now, for the moment, to date, until now, so far*

*by now* means ‘given everything that has happened before’. It is not commonly used in research papers.

- It should, **by now**, be well known that publications in peer-reviewed journals are more likely to ensure success than ...
- The literature on this topic should, **by now**, be extremely familiar to ...

*for now* and *for the moment* both mean ‘from this point in time until some time in the future when a change is expected’. *for now* and *for the moment* are generally followed either by the present simple or *will****.***

- We wanted to buy new equipment, but we do not have the funds, so **for now / for the moment** we will have to continue using our old equipment.
- We shall expand more fully on this in Sect. 3. **For now**, we just focus on ...

*Until now* and *so far* both mean ‘from a certain point in the past up to the present moment’. They imply, however, that something is likely to change. *To date* means the same, but without any hint that something might change. All three are usually used with the present perfect**.**

- **So far / Until now**, research into this area has been limited to X. In this paper, we investigate Y.
- This is the only acid that has **so far / until now** been found to be effective in such scenarios.

*Until now* and *to date* are not normally used directly before a past participle.

- The research **undertaken so far** ~~**/ so far undertaken**~~ has only focused on ...
- The patients **described so far /** ~~**so far described**~~ all had benign non-calcified nodules. Let us now turn to cases with calcified nodules.

Note *till*, which means the same as *until*, is considered too informal for research manuscripts.

#### 2.13 *during, over* and *throughout* (time)

*over* often refers to a period of time that began in the past and is still true in the present.

*over* is thus normally used with the present perfect. However, *over* can also be used for a future period.

- **Over** the last few years, there has been increasing interest in ... <small>= For the last few years, i.e. until and including today</small>
- **Over** the last decade, no progress has been made in ... However, **over** the next few years this will certainly change.

*during* means at some point/s in the course of a period of time. This period can either be in the past or future.

- I hope to have the opportunity of meeting you **during** the conference next month.
- I worked with him **during** my Erasmus project.

*throughout* means for the entire course of a period of time. This period can refer to the past, present or future.

- The levels were checked **throughout** the experiment. <small>= For the duration of the experiment.</small>
- Plagues were common **throughout** the Middle Ages. <small>= For the entire Middle Ages.</small>
- Plagues were common **during** the Middle Ages. <small>= At various points in the Middle Ages, but not necessarily for the entire period.</small>

#### 2.14 *for, since, from* (time)

*since* indicates the starting point of a current situation. It is typically used with precise points in time, e.g. *2023, last month, yesterday*. *since* answers the question ‘when did this situation begin?’ *since* is used with the present perfect, not the present simple**.**

- **Since** ~~**/ From**~~ 2023 there **has been** ~~**/ is**~~ a dramatic increase in suicides.

*for* indicates the duration from the past until the present. It is typically used with plural words indicating time, e.g. *days, months, years, decades*. *for* answers the question ‘how long has this situation been ongoing?’ In this sense, *for* is used with the present perfect**.**

- We **have been doing** ~~**/ do**~~ this research **for** ~~**/ from / since**~~ nine years.

If *for* is used to indicate a period of time that is now finished, then it is used with the simple past**.**

- I **studied** ~~**/ have studied**~~ in Boston **for** three years and **then** I moved to Beijing.

*from* indicates a range of time, i.e. with a start and finish. Because there is a finish time, *from* is not used with the present perfect. But *from* can be used with most other tenses.

- I **studied** ~~**/ have studied**~~ in Boston **from 2021 to 2024**.
- The biggest change **is likely to / will** happen **from 2050 to 2070.**

#### 2.15 *in, now, currently, at the moment, historically*

*in* is generally followed by a date (e.g. *in June, in 2030*) and is therefore not used with the present perfect. *in* can be used with the present, future, and the past.

- The new term **starts** in October.
- I **will see** you in March
- I **went** ~~**/ have been**~~ there in 2020.
- I **joined** ~~**/ have joined**~~ this research group **in July**.

*now, currently, at the moment* indicate a time period that is ongoing, so they are used with the present simple. On the other hand, *ago, yesterday, last week* (*month, year*) indicate a time that is completely past so they must be used with the simple past**.**

- ~~**Until last year this has been** the world’s biggest problem.~~
- This **is currently** the world’s biggest problem.

Adverbs that indicate a connection between the past and present are generally found with the **present perfect** (*historically, traditionally).*

- **Historically**, French **has always been / was always** taught in English schools as the second language.

#### 2.16 *in, inside, within* (location)

*in* and *inside* often have a similar meaning in relation to a confined space.

*within* means internal to something that can be a real physical space (e.g. *border*).

*inside* is the opposite of *outside*, *within* is never used in this sense.

- The money is kept **in / inside** ~~**/ within**~~ the safe.
- The hostage was kept **in / inside** ~~**/ within**~~ the same room for more than three years.
- We examined the links among the parents of children **in** ~~**/ inside / within**~~ the school.
- The children were only allowed to play **inside** ~~**/ in / within**~~ the school, never outside.
- Insufficient attention has been given to the importance of relationships among children **within** ~~**/ in / inside**~~ the school.

*within* can also be used with non-physical spaces (e.g. *confines, framework, comprehension*).

*inside* has a metaphorical meaning of ‘revelations’ regarding something, and is often found in paper titles (as in the last example below).

- The paper draws on research in six EU member states carried out **within** ~~**/ in / inside**~~ the framework of a project on climate change policies.
- Our aim is to present, **within** ~~**/ in / inside**~~ the limits of national security, a comprehensive summary of this data regarding the war in Ukraine.
- **Inside** ~~**/ In / Within**~~ the mind of a monkey: an investigation into anthropoid behavior.

#### 2.17 *of* and *with* (materials, methods, agreements)

*of* indicates a material out of which something is made.

*with* explains how something is created or what something is equipped with.

- Nitonol is an alloy **of** nickel and titanium.
- A cake can be made **with** various ingredients.
- These cars are manufactured **with** armor plating and come equipped **with** bullet- proof windows.

*made of* - discrete and clearly separated items.

*made up of* – focus on the whole thing rather than its different constituent elements.

- The royal family were wearing jewels made **of** gold and silver.
- Snow is made **up of** small crystals of ice.
- The reviewing committee is made **up of** six members.

*with* indicates the presence or absence of a relationship, agreement or support and is used with the following verbs, nouns and adjectives: *accordance, acquaint, agree, ally, appointment, associate, coincide, collaborate, comparable\*, compliance, comply, concur, connect\*, connection\*, consistent, contact, contaminate, cooperate, cooperation, coordinate, coordination, coupled, cover, deal, dispense, endow, entrust, equip, experiment, help, incompatible, incongruous, infect, interact, interfere, liaise, mix, paint, problem, provide, reinforce, synchronize, synchronous, tally* \[\* indicates that these words can also be followed by *to*\].

- The paper does not comply **with** the journal's formatting rules.
- The terrain was covered **with** snow.
- The severity of the illness varies **with** age.
