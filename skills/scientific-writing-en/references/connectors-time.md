### Writing about the Past

Writing about the past in English involves choosing from the rather complex tense system. The phrases grouped below give an indication of the uses of the main tenses in academic writing. For a comprehensive explanation of the uses of the various tenses you will need to consult an English grammar book. A good recommendation is *Practical English Usage* by Michael Swan, Oxford University Press.

#### Describing research history with past tense constructions

- The link between X and Y was established in 2000 by Smith *et al*.
- The association between X and Y was not demonstrated until 2012.
- Prior to the work of Smith (1983), the role of X was largely unknown.
- Before 1950, the X had received only cursory attention from historians.
- The construct of X was first articulated by Smith (1977) and popularized in his book: …
- It was not until the late 1960s that historians considered X worthy of scholarly attention.
- Awareness of X is not recent, having possibly first been described in the 5th century BCE by …
- The next research period involved innovative laboratory work in the late 1960s and into the 1970s.

#### Time phrases associated with the use of the present perfect tense: past and present connected

- *To date*, little evidence has been found associating X with Y.
- *Up to now*, the research has tended to focus on X rather than on Y.
- It is only *since* the work of Smith (2001) that the study of X has gained momentum.
- *So far*, three factors have been identified as being potentially important: X, Y, and Z.
- *Since* 1965, these four economies have doubled their share of world production and trade.

- *Until recently*, there has been little interest in X.
- *Only in the past ten years* have studies of X directly addressed how …
- *Recently*, these questions have been addressed by researchers in many fields.
- *In recent years*, researchers have investigated a variety of approaches to X but ...
- *More recently*, literature has emerged that offers contradictory findings about ...

- *Over the past century* there has been a dramatic increase in ...
- *The past decade* has seen the rapid development of X in many ...
- *Over the past 30 years* there has been a significant increase in ...
- *Over the past two decades*, major advances in molecular biology have allowed …
- *Over the past few decades*, the world has seen the stunning transformation of X, Y and Z.

- Several studies have revealed that ...
- Previous studies of X have not dealt with ...
- X has been intensively investigated recently due to its …

- X has been identified as major contributing factors for the decline of ....

- The new material has been shown to enhance X (Smith, 1985, Jones, 1987).
- The relationship between X and Y has been widely investigated (Smith, 1985, Jones, ...
- There have been several investigations into the causes of X (Smith, 1985; Jones, 1987).

- In 1981, Smith and co-workers demonstrated that X induced in vitro resistance to ...
- In 1990, Jones *et al.* demonstrated that replacement of H<sub>2</sub>O with heavy water led to ...

### 7 Time

#### 7.1.1 Dates

In the short date format, the order of the day and month are reversed in British and US English. Therefore, when writing dates, use the longer format to avoid ambiguity, for example 4 July 1776:

- **British English**
- 4-7-1776
- **US English**
- 7-4-1776
- **Better**
- 4 July 1776 or July 4, 1776

If you wish, you can add *-st*, *-nd*, *-rd*, *th* after the date, for example *1st*, *2nd*, *3rd*, *4th* July. However, this is not necessary. The date format with superscript *1<sup>st</sup>*, *2<sup>nd</sup>*, *3<sup>rd</sup>* or *4<sup>th</sup>* is now somewhat old-fashioned.

#### 7.1.2 Years

Decades and centuries can be written without an apostrophe:

- The 1900s saw great changes in many countries.
- The 1960s were a time of great social change.

Unless you use the abbreviated form, in which case an apostrophe should be added before the date:

- The ’60s were a time of great social change.
- The ’40s saw widespread famine.

However, this abbreviated form is more informal and potentially ambiguous. In university and research writing, therefore, it is better to write years in full.

When referring to year ranges, if you abbreviate, abbreviate sensibly so that the meaning is clear.

#### 7.1.3 BC and AD

Where used, BC is written after a date and AD before a date in formal writing, for example:

|  |  |
|---|---|
| 55 BC | AD 1066 |
| 25,000 BC | AD 1564 |

However, as BC means ‘Before Christ’ and AD is short for ‘Anno Domini’, which means ‘In the Year of the Lord’, you may prefer BCE and CE respectively as more neutral alternatives. BCE means *Before the Common Era*, and CE means *Common Era*.

#### 7.1.4 Time

Beware of using relative terms, which vary depending on when your work is written or read, for example:

- in the last five years
- **Better:** from 2000–2005
- next year
- **Better:** in 2008

This is particularly the case for texts with a long time span, such as a thesis or a book.

In writing times, British English usually uses a full stop to separate hours and minutes, while US English uses a colon, for example:

- 7.30 (British English)
- 7:30 (US English)

If you use the 12-hour clock, *a.m.* means before noon, and *p.m.* means after noon:

- Breakfast is served from 7 a.m. to 9 a.m.
- Dinner is at 7 p.m.

   - a. Have you used any relative terms? If so, are they OK?
3. In class, in groups or in pairs, exchange texts and evaluate each other’s writing, going through the points above

### 6 Link words

#### 6.1 *about, as far as ... is concerned*

Do not use *about* at the beginning of a sentence to introduce a topic.

- **Concerning / Regarding / On the subject of / With regard to** ~~**/ About**~~ the paper we sent you in May, we would like to know whether ...
- We are writing to you **about** the paper we sent you in May. We would like to

A*s far as x is concerned* is used to introduce a new topic in which the dependent phrase has a subject that is different from the topic introduced in the previous phrase.

Avoid unnecessary or excessive use of *as far as x is concerned*. It can often be rewritten in a more concise form.

The sentences in red below are not incorrect, just verbose.

- ~~**As far as the budget is concerned**, **this** can be discussed at the next meeting.~~ <small>*budget* is the subject of both phrases.</small>
- The **budget** can be discussed at the next meeting.
- **As far as the budget is concerned**, **we** would to ask you whether ... <small>*we* is the subject of the second phrase.</small>
- ~~**As far as the second phase is concerned,** we can draw a similar conclusion as for the first phase.~~
- **We can draw** a similar conclusion for the second phase as for the first phase.

#### 6.2 *also, in addition, as well, besides, moreover*

*In addition, also, further, furthermore* - to add an additional positive or neutral comment.

- This software program has several interesting features .... **In addition / Also / Furthermore** ~~**/ Moreover**~~, the cost is low and it is quick to learn.

*Moreover* – to add a negative comment – this is not a rule, but seems to be a preference among native English-speaking authors.

- This software program has no useful features. **Moreover**, the cost is very high and it takes a long time to learn.

*Besides* and *in addition to* (both + −*ing* form) are used at the beginning of a sentence which is made up of two parts, in which the second part contains an additional feature or fact to the one given in the first part. *besides* is not used at the beginning of sentence to add an additional idea to the one presented in a previous sentence.

- **Besides / In addition to** having several interesting features, this program is also economical ...
- This software program has several interesting features .... **In addition** ~~**/ Besides**~~, the cost is low and it is quick to learn.

*As well (as)* means the same as *also*. *as well as* can be used at the beginning of a phrase and takes the -*ing* form of the verb. *as well*, but not *also*, can be used at the end of the phrase.

- **In addition to / Besides / As well as** teach**ing** English, she **also** teaches French.
- In addition to / Besides / As well as **teaching** ~~**/ to teach**~~ English, she also teaches French.
- She teaches French **as well** ~~**/ also**~~.

#### 6.3 *also, as well, too, both, all:* use with *not*

*also*, *as well as, too, both* and *all* are not generally used in negative sentences. Instead, they need to be replaced with other words, as outlined below.

Use *neither* / *nor* instead of *also, as well as, too.*

- ~~X did **not** function and **also** Y.~~
- X did **not** function and **nor / neither** did Y.
- Little is known about what truly matters in searching for information, **nor** ~~**/ as well as**~~ what strategies users exploit.

Use *either / neither* instead of *both.*

- We did **not** use **either** ~~/ **both**~~ of them, just one of them.
- ~~**Both** of them **did not function** as required.~~
- **Neither** of them **functioned** as required.

Use *any* instead of *all.*

- There were no high scores in **any** ~~**/ all**~~ of the tests.

#### 6.4 *although, even though* vs *even if*

*Even though* and *although* have the same meaning. They are used to refer to real situations. They are generally found with present tenses. *though* means the same, but is not found at the beginning of a sentence in academic writing; *even though* is generally found at the beginning of a sentence rather than the middle.

- **Even though / Although** ~~**/ Though**~~ researchers don’t earn much money, at least they get to travel a lot.
- **Even though / Although** ~~**/ Though**~~ the book is essentially for children, adults still love to read it.

*Even if* is only used for hypothetical situations. Note: ~~*also if*~~ does not exist.

- **Even if** ~~**/ Also if**~~ I had Elon Musk’s money, I would never buy a ...
- **Even if** ~~**/ Even though / Also if**~~ the book were available in English (it is currently only in Spanish), nobody would read it.

#### 6.5 *and, along with* – avoiding ambiguity

In a list of three items or more, put a comma before *and* – this signals to the reader that the *and* is introducing the last item.

- These countries include Tajikistan, Uzbekistan**, and** Kyrgyzstan.

When giving a list of items, use semi colons (or commas) to highlight what elements *and* joins together.

- The following groups of countries will be involved in the project: Tunisia and Egypt**;** Vietnam and Laos**;** Peru and Chile**;** and Poland and Estonia.

When you use *and* several times within the same phrase this can cause ambiguity. Rephrase the sentence. Alternatively, use *along with* or *together with* to make your meaning clear.

- ~~I could visit your lab in **January and February and March** if my professor agrees.~~ <small>Do I need to ask prof’s permission just for March, or for February and March, or for all three months?</small>
- I could visit your lab in **January**. I could also come in **February and March** if my professor agrees.
- ~~A **and** B **and** C **and** D are the most used solutions.~~
- A and B, **along with** C and D, are the most used solutions.

*Along with* is followed by a noun.

- **Along with / Besides / In addition to** Spanish and Chinese, English is the most spoken language in the world.

#### 6.6 *as* versus *as it*

When *as* is used without a following pronoun or noun, it has a similar meaning to *like* and *how.*

- This is not true, **as is** ~~**/ as it is**~~ evident from the figure.
- **As mentioned** above and **as can** ~~**/ as it can**~~ be seen in the figure ...

*as* means *because* or *since,* when it is followed with a pronoun (often *it*) or a noun.

- ~~This is not true, **as is** impossible to prove that X=Y.~~
- This is not true, **as it / because it / since it** is impossible to prove that X=Y.
- These experiments were not performed, **as it / because it / since it** would have required too much additional computing power.

#### 6.7 *as* vs *like (unlike)*

*As* = one thing is equal to another.

*Like* = ‘similar to’.

- He works **as** ~~**/ like**~~ a researcher in Paris. <small>He is a researcher.</small>
- She works **like** ~~**/ as**~~ a slave for her boss. <small>She is not a slave.</small>
- Diabetes acts **as** a significant risk factor for many physical diseases. <small>Diabetes is a risk factor.</small>
- Xerostima: A symptom that acts **like** ~~**/ as**~~ a disease. <small>Xerostima, i.e. dry mouth resulting from absent saliva flow, is not a disease but a symptom that can lead to a disease.</small>

*Unlike* is used when making a contrast. Note ~~*differently from*~~ does not exist, use *unlike* instead.

- Zinc, **unlike** ~~**/ differently from**~~ copper and iron, fails to stimulate lipid peroxidation in vitro.
- <small>Zinc does not behave in the same way as copper and iron.</small>

#### 6.8 *because, why, due to, owing to, since, as*

*Because* indicates a consequence, *why* gives the reason or explanations.

- This battery may explode when used with a third-party power supply. This is **because** the battery is highly inflammable and this is **why** it should not be used in children’s toys.

*Due to* and *owing to* mean the same as *because of*, and are followed by a noun. *Owing to* tends only to be used at the beginning of a sentence.

- This battery may explode when used with a third-party power supply. This is **due to the fact that** ~~**/ owing to the fact**~~ the battery is highly inflammable and this is **the reason \[why\]** it should not be used in children’s toys.

*Because* can be used at the beginning of a sentence in order to explain a reason for doing something, but is usually replaced in formal English by *since, as, seeing as, given that, given the fact that*, *on account of the fact that* or *due to the fact that.*

- **Because / Due to the fact that / Owing to the fact / On account of the fact that / Given that / Since** the battery is highly inflammable it should not be used in children’s toys.
- **Because / As / Since / Given that / On account of the fact that** they wanted total control, the revolutionary party enacted a series of drastic reforms.

#### 6.9 *both ... and, either ... or*

If these expressions are misused they cause ambiguity.

*Both ... and* is inclusive.

- ~~We can go **either to** Iran **either** / **and to** Jordon.~~
- We can go to **both** Iran **and** Jordon. <small>We will visit two places.</small>

*Either ... or* is exclusive.

- ~~We can go **or to** Iran **or to** Jordon.~~
- We can go to **either** Iran **or** Jordon. <small>We can only visit one of the two alternatives.</small>

*Both* is only used with *not* when used to make a contrast. *Not ... either ..*. *or* indicates that none of the options are available

- We ca**n’t** go to **both** Iran and Jordon, but only to Iran. <small>We only have one choice.</small>
- We ca**n’t** go **either** to Iran **or** Jordon. <small>We cannot visit Iran and we cannot visit Jordon.</small>

The position of the preposition changes the meaning.

- We had fun **in both** the parks we visited, and also the museums. <small>We visited two parks.</small>
- We had fun **both in** the parks and the museums. <small>We visited an undisclosed number of parks and museums.</small>

#### 6.10 *e.g., i.e.,* and *etc.*

##### form

Although *e.g. i.e.* and *etc.* are Latin forms they are not written in italics.

*e.g.* and *i.e.* are often written *eg* and *ie*, but this may look confusing.

No comma is required after *e.g.* and *i.e.*

- This is true in at least ten countries, **e.g.** Spain, Japan and Togo.
- ~~This is true in at least ten countries, ***e.g*****.** Spain, Japan and Togo.~~
- ~~This is true in at least ten countries, ***eg*** Spain, Japan and Togo.~~
- ~~This is true in at least ten countries, **e.g.,** Spain, Japan and Togo.~~

##### usage

*e.g.* introduces an example of what you have just said.

Use *i.e.* when what follows is a definition or clarification of what you have just said.

*e.g.* and *i.e.* are often confused. If you think your readers might not be familiar with the difference use *for example* and *that is to say*, respectively.

- This is true in at least ten countries, **for example / e.g**~~**./ i.e.**~~ Spain, Japan and Togo.
- The UK is made up of four countries, **that is to say / i.e.** ~~**/ e.g.**~~ England, Scotland, Wales and N. Ireland.

When you introduce a series of examples with *for example*, do not put *etc.* at the end.

- This is true in at least ten countries, **e.g.** Spain, Japan, Togo~~, **etc**.~~

If possible, think of something more meaningful than *etc.*

- ~~This is true in many nations (Honduras **etc.**) and has very serious consequences.~~
- This is true in many nations (Honduras **and other Central American countries**) and has very serious consequences.

#### 6.11 *e.g.* vs *for example*

Use a comma before *for example* and at the end of the example itself.

- Whenever you use your **PIN, for example** to get money from an **ATM,** do not let anyone see you.

If you write *for example* after the example, rather than before, then it should be preceded and followed by commas. *for example* should not be placed at the end of the phrase.

- ~~Many governments are in crisis. In Venezuela the government is facing big problems with the unions, **for example.**~~
- Many governments are in crisis. In **Venezuela, for example, the** government is facing ...

In the middle of a sentence *e.g.* tends to be used for lists that are in brackets.

- ~~When you use a PIN **e.g**. to get money from an ATM, to pay for online purchases ensure that ...~~
- When you use a PIN **(e.g.** to get money from an ATM, to pay for online purchases) ensure that ...

*like,* when giving an example*,* is not normally used in research papers, prefer *for example* and *such as.*

- We have collaborations with universities in many countries in Europe, **for example / such as** ~~**/ like**~~ France and Spain.

Don’t use both *such as* and *for example* together. Use one or the other.

- ~~We have collaborations with universities in Europe, **such as for example** France and Spain.~~

#### 6.12 *for this reason* vs *for this purpose, to this end*

*for this reason* explains why something was done.

- They wish to improve their English. **For this reason**, they are studying ten hours a day.
- The patient was suffering from amnesia, **for this reason** it was difficult to question her directly on the circumstances of the accident.

*for this purpose* and *to this end* can be used indifferently to describe how something just mentioned was achieved.

- Our aim was to achieve higher performance. **For this purpose / To this end** we built an ad hoc device to provide increased power.
- It is now considered expedient to purge bone marrow of tumor cells prior to returning it to the patient, and **to this end / for this purpose** a variety of techniques have been developed.

#### 6.13 *the former, the latter*

Only use *the former* and *the latter* when it is 100% clear to the reader what *the former* and *the latter* refer *to.* In the example below, there is no doubt for the reader that *the latter* refers to Turkey because there are only two countries involved.

- There are two countries involved: Georgia and Turkey. **The latter** is ...

However, when revising your paper if your professor or colleague decided the switch the order of *Georgia* and *Turkey* (i.e. if they wrote *Turkey and Georgia*), they might forget to change *the latter* to *the former*. By always replacing *the former* and *the latter* with the word they refer to, there can never be any problems.

- There are two countries involved in the project: Turkey and Georgia. **Georgia /** ~~**The latter**~~ is ...

It is not bad style to repeat the key word, particularly as this will make it easier for the reader to identify exactly what is being referred to. In long sentences the reader may have already forgotten which elements were mentioned earlier.

- There are two countries involved in the project: Turkey and Georgia. In fact, originally many countries expressed interest. However, these countries blah blah blah blah blah. **Turkey /** ~~**The latter**~~ is ...
- In this recipe we used potatoes, carrots and beans. This is common practice with this kind of cooking. **The beans** ~~**/ The latter**~~ can, of course, be steamed.
- **Lagos** and Khartoum are the capital cities of Nigeria and Sudan. They are both heavily populated with a population of around 16 and 6 million, respectively. Blah blah blah blah. **Lagos** ~~**/ The former**~~ has a population of ...

#### 6.14 *however, although, but, yet, despite, nevertheless*

To qualify what you have just written, use *however* (or *but*, which is slightly less formal). *however* is used in preference to *but* at the beginning of a sentence. *however* can be used with or without a comma, and can be located mid phrase between two commas. *nonetheless* and *nevertheless* are synonyms and mean the same as *however.*

- The system costs very little to implement, **but / however / nevertheless / although** it is very complicated to use.
- The system is cheap. **However / Nevertheless**, it is very complicated to use.

*Yet* means the same as *but* and *however*, but has a stronger note of surprise. *still* has a similar meaning.

- Governments know this is a problem, **yet** ~~**/ despite**~~ they do nothing about it.
- **Although** governments know this is a problem, they **still** do nothing about it.

Consider avoiding the use of *despite* and *notwithstanding* as they sometimes require a more complex grammatical construction.

- ~~The system costs very little to implement, **despite / notwithstanding** it is very complicated to use.~~
- The system costs very little to implement. It is, **however / nevertheless**, very complicated to use.
- **Although** ~~**/ Despite / Notwithstanding**~~ the system is cheap, it works well.
- The system works well, **however** ~~**/ notwithstanding**~~ it is rather complicated.
- **Despite** being cheap, the system works well.
- **Despite the fact / Notwithstanding the fact that** the system is cheap, it is very effective.

#### 6.15 *in contrast with* vs *compared to, by comparison with*

Use *in contrast with* when the difference you are referring to is striking or surprising.

- **In contrast to** what was previously observed by Heimlich \[2\], our results showed an opposite trend.
- **In contrast to** Hill’s top-down approach \[Hill, 2025\], we start from the bottom layer.

Use *compared to* / *with* and *by comparison* in all other cases.

- **Compared to** Smith’s results, our results are somewhat disappointing.
- Our results are somewhat disappointing **by comparison with** Smith’s.
- **Compared to** the old technology, the new technology offers several new features.

#### 6.16 *instead, on the other hand, whereas, on the contrary*

Use *instead* at the beginning of a sentence to resolve a problem stated in the previous sentence.

*On the contrary* is used to totally contradict what another author has stated.

- Do not join two independent clauses with a semicolon. **Instead** ~~/ **On the contrary,**~~ make two simple separate sentences.
- Reale et al (2026) claim that the British are in favor of the monarchy. **On the contrary**, we found that most Britons would like to abolish the monarchy, as it is anachronistic and a waste of taxpayers money,

Do not use *instead* to introduce a new topic, even if the new topic is related in some way to the previous topic. Use *on the other hand.*

- Italian and Spanish are similar languages, in fact they both derive from Latin. **On the other hand** ~~**/ Instead / On the contrary**~~**,** German is derived from ...

Use *on the other hand* to give an alternative or to add additional information about the thing mentioned previously. Use *whereas* to make a contrast.

- The conference may be held in Jordon, **on the other hand** ~~**/ whereas**~~ it may be held in Egypt.
- This year the conference is being held in Prague, **whereas** ~~**/ on the other hand**~~ last year it was held on the other side of the globe in Sydney.
- Italian and Spanish are similar languages, **whereas** German is completely different.

*Whereas* is not normally used at the beginning of a sentence.

- Italian and Spanish are similar languages. **On the other hand** ~~**/ Whereas**~~ German is completely different.

#### 6.17 *that, which, who, whose*

Use *that* and *which* for things, and *who* for people. Note the comma (,) in the example below – it highlights that *who* is introducing additional information about Scott.

- Apple’s first CEO was Michael Scott, **who** ~~**/ that / which**~~ ran the company from 1977 to 1982.

After a preposition, use *which* (things) and *whom* (people). Note the word order.

- I have several mobile phones, many **of which** ~~**/ that**~~ don’t work.
- This institute employs many people, most **of whom** ~~**/ who**~~ are technicians.

If you put an adjective after the noun it describes, then this adjective should normally be introduced by *that*, *which* or *who.*

- I met a student **who is** 25 years old. She wrote a **document which / that is** five pages long / ~~She wrote a **document five** pages long.~~

*whose* indicates possession.

- Professor Shirov, **whose** ~~**/ who’s / of who**~~ seminal paper was published in 2023, is professor of ...

#### 6.18 *that* vs *which* and *who*

*that* – to define the preceding noun in order to differentiate it from another noun or define it in some way (i.e. a restrictive sense). In the examples below *which* and *who* would be OK in general English, but might be ambiguous in scientific English.

- I work with Professor Smith, **who** ~~**/ that**~~ teaches economics at LSE.
- I collaborate with the Professor Smith **that** ~~**/ who**~~ teaches economics, not the Professor Smith **that** teaches sociology.
- Google has many offices. I work for the office **that** ~~**/ which**~~ is in London.

*which, who* – to add parenthetical information about the preceding noun (i.e. a non-restrictive sense). Such parenthetical information is not essential to the sentence – if this information was removed the sentence would still make sense. In such cases you are not differentiating the noun, you are simply giving further details. The subordinate clause in which *who* and *which* occurs is generally separated by commas.

- Google, **which** ~~**/ that**~~ is a huge company, receives thousands of CVs every day.
- Professor Jones, **who** ~~**/ that**~~ lectures in political sciences, is 45 years old.

*which, who* – to add additional information at the end of sentence. Again, a comma is required (6.17).

- Google sells a lot of advertising, **which** ~~**/ that**~~ is one way the company gets its money.
- I work with Professor Ling, **who** ~~**/ that**~~ I have known for several years.

#### 6.19 *thus, therefore, hence, consequently, so, thereby*

*thus, therefore, consequently, so* and *hence* all have the same meaning. They are used to indicate a consequence of what has just been said before. *so* is considered informal and is thus used less often.

- Researchers do not have much time to read papers. **Consequently / Therefore / Thus** ~~**/ So**~~, it makes sense to write papers in a way that they can understand quickly and easily.
- Researchers do not have much time to read papers. The best way to write a paper is **thus** to use short sentences.

*hence* is generally reserved for mathematics.

- Note that the right-hand side of equation (2) equals r(p)v(x)+\[3\]. **Hence**, equation (2) reduces to equation (1) if ...

*thereby* means *in such a way*. It can only be used in the dependent phrase and is followed by a verb.

- Love promotes well-being **thus / thereby** enabling people to live better lives.
